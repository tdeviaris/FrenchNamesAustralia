During the Baudin expedition, François Péron showed a sustained interest in the pinnipeds encountered along the Australian coasts. His observations were not confined to the elephant seals of King Island: he also studied other populations, notably on Kangaroo Island and in Tasmania, while comparing the names used by navigators, sealers and naturalists with the anatomical characteristics of the animals.

This attention illustrates the close relationship between geography and natural history that characterised voyages of discovery around the turn of the nineteenth century. Hydrographic reconnaissance of the coasts went hand in hand with the inventorying of species, their graphic representation, their classification, and the assessment of the resources they might provide. The place names themselves preserve traces of this encounter between maritime practice and scientific enquiry.

From maritime names to zoological classification

The vocabulary encountered in the expedition’s narratives, journals and cartography is particularly varied: phoque, éléphant marin, loup marin, chien marin, ours marin, alongside the more scientific term otarie. These names did not form a uniform vernacular system. They derived from different maritime and linguistic traditions and could refer to different animals or emphasise particular aspects of their appearance.

Péron sought to bring greater order to this terminological diversity by relying on anatomy. In particular, he distinguished the eared seals, which possess external ear flaps, from the true seals, which lack them. The transition from vocabulary inherited from navigators and hunters to a classification based on morphological characteristics thus forms one of the recurring themes of his observations.

The elephant seal of King Island

In his study of the elephant seal, Péron reviews earlier names and points out what he regarded as their shortcomings. In particular, he criticises Linnaeus’s use of the name Leonina, which he considered to have been based on an inaccurate description.

He also records an Indigenous name for the animal, which he transcribes as Miourong. This term is the source of the modern generic name Mirounga. Its precise linguistic origin, however, remains difficult to establish on the basis of the available sources.

Péron also explains the English designation Sea-Elephant. He notes that British sealers on King Island used this name for the animal, and that the bay where these mammals gathered in large numbers had consequently received the name Baie des Éléphants. This designation, of English origin, should be distinguished from the French expedition toponyms proper, notably Baie des Phoques and Rocher des Éléphants.

Considering the existing names insufficiently precise for zoological purposes, Péron proposed the designation “Phoque à trompe” (Phoca proboscidea), referring to the characteristic nasal prolongation of the male. This belongs to his historical zoological nomenclature; the species is now known as Mirounga leonina.

He was also interested in the species’ distribution and seasonal movements, which he interpreted as migrations between the higher southern latitudes and more northerly shores. His own observations were supplemented by information obtained from the British sealers established on the island.

Finally, Péron emphasised what he regarded as the particularly peaceful disposition of these animals, describing them as “extremely gentle and tractable”. He maintained that they did not spontaneously attack humans and even recounted the story of an elephant seal tamed by one of the English sealers. The contrast between the gentleness he attributed to the animal and the violence of its commercial exploitation gives his account particular force.

Kangaroo Island: observing and drawing another species

On Kangaroo Island, the presence of pinnipeds also left its mark on French nomenclature in the form of Anse des Phoques, corresponding to present-day Western Cove.

Natural-history observations made on the island nevertheless showed that the animals encountered did not all belong to the same species. Péron and Lesueur studied another pinniped there, which they classified among the eared seals under the name Otaria cinerea. This species is now known as the Australian sea lion, Neophoca cinerea, for which Kangaroo Island is the historical type locality.

Lesueur’s drawing of this animal is particularly revealing of the expedition’s natural-history methods. Executed in watercolour and pencil, it retains numerous preparatory annotations intended to record the animal’s appearance with precision. Lesueur noted, for example, the colours to be applied — “grey” for the neck, “reddish grey” for the torso, and “black arm” for the foreflipper — and separately drew certain anatomical details, including the teeth. Other notes concern the tail, its covering of hair, and various features of the body.

The drawing should therefore be understood not simply as a finished natural-history illustration, but as a genuine record of scientific observation. Sketches, colour indications, anatomical notes and morphological details all contribute to the description and zoological identification of the animal. Lesueur’s graphic work was thus an integral part of the natural-history investigation conducted during the voyage.

Tasmania: Îlot des Phoques and the “sea bears”

The presence of pinnipeds also appears in the French nomenclature of Tasmania’s eastern coast with Îlot des Phoques, now Ile des Phoques, off the Freycinet Peninsula.

Freycinet provides a description that closely combines coastal geography, hydrography and natural-history observation:

“Îlot des Phoques, situated two leagues to the east of the mainland, is a barren and rocky islet. We saw it covered with an innumerable quantity of sea bears. Soundings at two boat-lengths to the west gave 14 fathoms, and 30 fathoms at less than a mile’s distance.”

The islet is therefore described both as an isolated rocky feature, surrounded by waters that deepen rapidly, and as an important gathering place for pinnipeds.

The expression “sea bear” introduces another term from the vernacular vocabulary of the period. It was used in particular for certain fur seals and did not therefore necessarily refer to the same species as the “proboscis seal” observed on King Island.

The succession of terms — phoque, otarie, éléphant marin, loup marin, chien marin, ours marin — thus reflects both the diversity of the pinnipeds encountered and the plurality of lexical traditions with which the travellers had to contend.

From southern Australia to Shark Bay: Baie des Chiens-Marins

This terminological diversity was not confined to the southern coasts. On the western coast of Australia, Shark Bay was known to the French as Baie des Chiens-Marins.

The designation is attested in the French cartographic tradition associated with Saint-Aloüarn’s reconnaissance in 1772, and was later retained in Freycinet’s atlas. The expression chien marin likewise belonged to an older maritime vocabulary for marine mammals.

A comparison of these different place names therefore suggests not an incoherent succession of labels, but rather the coexistence of vernacular traditions that the expedition’s naturalists sought to place within a more ordered scientific framework.

Pinnipeds as a maritime resource

Péron’s interest in these animals also had an important economic dimension. On King Island, he observed an already organised exploitation of elephant seals by British sealers, who rendered their blubber for oil and also traded their skins.

The establishments he encountered were not merely temporary camps. They formed part of maritime networks linking Bass Strait, Port Jackson and more distant markets, particularly in Asia. Péron was especially attentive to the speed with which the British had transformed the animal resources of these shores into organised commercial activities.

This observation took on particular significance in the context of Franco-British maritime competition. Without presenting Péron as the explicit spokesman for a French economic programme, his account reveals a clear interest in the profits generated by these new sealing grounds and in the advantage already acquired by their British operators.

Yet his economic assessment of the resource was accompanied by reflection on the limits of its exploitation. Péron recognised that the pressure exerted by sealers could rapidly deplete the very populations on which their activity depended. In his study of the elephant seal, human beings thus emerge as the most dangerous predators, capable through intensive hunting of bringing about the local disappearance of the species.

This tension between economic usefulness and the vulnerability of the resource is particularly revealing of concerns at the beginning of the nineteenth century. The exploitation of the natural world and its scientific study were not yet conceived as entirely separate spheres: both formed part of a broader enterprise of knowledge, assessment and appropriation of the territories through which the expedition travelled.

Animals at the intersection of geography, science and economy

Taken together, the toponyms Baie des Phoques, Rocher des Éléphants, Anse des Phoques, Îlot des Phoques and Baie des Chiens-Marins show how prominent pinnipeds were in the French experience of the Australian coasts.

They highlight several dimensions characteristic of early nineteenth-century voyages of discovery: the reconnaissance and naming of coastlines, observation of the living world, natural-history drawing, zoological classification, assessment of resources, and study of the economic networks developing across the southern seas.

For Péron and Lesueur, pinnipeds thus became objects at once geographical, zoological and economic. Their presence contributed to the naming of coasts; their anatomy informed scientific classification; their representation drew upon the methods of scientific illustration; and their exploitation revealed the rapid transformation of newly surveyed maritime spaces into hunting and trading grounds.

This convergence of hydrography, natural history and economy makes their study particularly representative of the encyclopaedic ambitions of voyages of discovery in the Napoleonic era.
