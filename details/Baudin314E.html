Cap Bonpland - Stokes Point

In Paris in 1798, Aimé Jacques Alexandre Bonpland (1773-1858) was selected as a naturalist for the projected voyage around the world entrusted to Nicolas Baudin. Alexander von Humboldt (1769-1859), who was also considering joining the expedition, met Bonpland during these preparations. Humboldt later recalled that Bonpland, who had trained under botanists Antoine-Laurent de Jussieu and René Desfontaines (see their toponyms), had been designated, together with André Michaux, to accompany the expedition as a naturalist. When the departure was postponed, Humboldt and Bonpland eventually abandoned the project and undertook their own voyage of exploration.

From 1799 to 1804, Humboldt and Bonpland travelled through the equinoctial regions of Spanish America, including territories now forming Venezuela, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru and Mexico, as well as Cuba. Their investigations combined geographical and physical observations with extensive botanical collecting. They assembled several thousand plants and specimens; Humboldt estimated that their collections contained approximately 1,400 to 1,500 species new to science.

On his return to France in 1804, Bonpland enjoyed the favour of the imperial regime: Napoleon granted him a pension, and he entered the service of Empress Joséphine, becoming one of the principal figures responsible for the botanical collections at Malmaison. He took part in the acclimatisation, cultivation and study of the many exotic plants assembled in the estate's gardens and greenhouses. Australian species were among those cultivated there, some grown from seeds brought back by the Baudin expedition. Bonpland described several of these plants in his Description des plantes rares cultivées à Malmaison et à Navarre, published between 1812 and 1817 and illustrated notably by Pierre-Joseph Redouté and Pancrace Bessa.

Following the fall of the Empire, Bonpland left Europe permanently in 1816 for South America. He settled in the Río de la Plata region and continued his botanical, medical and agricultural work for more than forty years, principally in territories now belonging to Argentina, Paraguay, Brazil and Uruguay.
