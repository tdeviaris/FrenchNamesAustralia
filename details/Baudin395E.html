Anse de l'Aiguade – Frenchman Bay

When Baudin arrived at King George Sound on 17 February 1803, he had been separated since 1 February from the schooner Casuarina and her commander, Louis de Freycinet. Freycinet was returning from his hydrographic mission in the two gulfs now known as Saint Vincent Gulf and Spencer Gulf, but had failed to rejoin the Géographe as expected. The Casuarina had in fact been sighted by the Géographe at around two o’clock in the afternoon, in the waters of Kangaroo Island, but Freycinet had not tacked in order to follow Baudin.

At King George Sound on 18 February 1803, Baudin sent his longboat to Anse de l’Aiguade to reconnoitre the watering place. The crew reported that the water was good and easy to collect, but also that they had found there ten casks belonging to the Casuarina. The schooner was then at anchor in Princess Charlotte Harbour, now Princess Royal Harbour. The reunion of the Géographe and the Casuarina, however, did not ease the tensions between Baudin and Freycinet, which are clearly apparent in the commander’s journal:

“When the water boat returned, I was informed that the water was very good and easy to collect. We found ten casks belonging to the Casuarina, which we knew must be in one of the two harbours and which had let us know that it had arrived before us by flying a flag on top of the Seals’ Islet. Our arrival had been announced to it the previous evening by a cannon shot fired during the night, to which it had replied with several rockets, informing us that it was in Princess Charlotte Harbour.

At about eight o’clock, we sighted the boat from this vessel and shortly afterwards Citizen Freycinet, who commanded it, arrived. When I asked him why he had not joined me when we met off Borda Island [Kangaroo Island], he replied that he had been unable to reach me and that, in any case, I should have waited for him. I then pointed out to him the poor manoeuvre he had made, which sailors would be able to judge for themselves, but he remained convinced that his own manoeuvre had been better than ours and that it was the sole cause of the separation of which I complained.”

More broadly, this episode reveals the tensions that ran through Baudin’s expedition. Both officers and savants often harboured deep mistrust, and at times resentment, toward the commander. The officers regarded Baudin as an outsider to the traditional corps of the French navy, questioning his seniority, his naval training and his earlier service under Austria.

The savants, too, had grievances against him. Immediate hardships — discipline, fatigue, privation, illness and uncertainty — certainly fuelled their discontent, but the sources of tension went deeper. The growing specialisation of scientific knowledge increasingly excluded amateurs, even experienced ones, from the legitimate sphere of science. Baudin, despite his experience in navigation and natural history collecting, was gradually marginalised by the expedition’s naturalists. This distance became particularly evident during the second phase of the voyage, especially at King George Sound, where the scientific work of the savants developed largely outside his direct authority.
