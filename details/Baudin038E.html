Cap Bernouilli - Cape Jaffa

Île Bernouilli - Bernouilli Island

The Bernoulli family, originally from Basel, formed one of the great European scientific dynasties of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Several of its members contributed to advances in mathematics, mechanics and the study of fluid motion. Their research had important implications for nautical science at a time when scientists were seeking to understand more precisely how ships behaved under the action of wind and water.

Johann Bernoulli (1667–1748), one of the principal advocates and developers of Leibniz’s differential and integral calculus, addressed these questions directly. In his Essai d’une nouvelle théorie de la manœuvre des vaisseaux (1714), he applied mathematical analysis to the forces exerted by the wind on sails and the resulting movements of ships. He sought, in particular, to explain how a vessel could make progress in different directions, including when the wind was not blowing in the direction of its intended course. This approach formed part of a fundamental transformation in nautical science: ship handling, previously based largely on the practical experience of seafarers, was also becoming a subject of mathematical investigation. The complexity of his calculations, however, limited their direct application by navigators.

His son Daniel Bernoulli (1700–1782) continued this investigation of mechanical phenomena. In his Hydrodynamica (1738), he examined the relationships between velocity, pressure and the motion of fluids, helping to establish the foundations of modern hydrodynamics. Together with the research of other eighteenth-century scientists, these studies gradually expanded the theoretical knowledge needed to understand the behaviour of ships in water.

By naming a cap and an island after the Bernoulli family, Péron and Freycinet thus honoured a scientific dynasty whose work had contributed to the theoretical foundations of nautical science. Although the navigators of the great voyages of discovery relied primarily on practical seamanship, instruments and hydrographic methods, their expeditions also formed part of a broader development of mathematical and physical sciences applied to navigation.
