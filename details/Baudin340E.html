Piton Faujas - Redpa Hill

Cap Faujas - Point Hillier

The work of Barthélemy Faujas de Saint-Fond belongs to a formative period in the history of the Earth sciences. In the late eighteenth century, geology was only beginning to emerge as an independent discipline, developing out of mineralogy, natural history and field observation. It was then shaped by a major debate between Neptunists and Plutonists. The former, represented above all by Abraham Gottlob Werner, explained the formation of most rocks as successive deposits laid down in the waters of an ancient universal ocean. Within this framework, basalt could be regarded as a rock of sedimentary origin. The latter, by contrast, emphasised the role of the Earth's internal heat, molten matter and volcanic activity in the formation of rocks.

Faujas's research on the extinct volcanoes of the Vivarais and Velay, in the Massif Central, helped strengthen this second interpretation. By demonstrating that the basalts of these regions were linked to ancient eruptive activity, he contributed to the recognition of volcanism as one of the major forces shaping the geological history of the Earth. His conclusions were consistent with those of his contemporary Déodat de Dolomieu, a French geologist also honoured in the expedition's nomenclature, who had studied volcanoes in Italy and Sicily and recognised the volcanic origin of basalt.

Freycinet or Boullanger may have recognised, or at least suspected, the volcanic nature of the hill they named Piton Faujas. This hypothesis finds some support in the geology of the Redpa region, where extensive basaltic formations resulting from ancient volcanic activity are exposed. The place name thus paid tribute to Barthélemy Faujas de Saint-Fond, one of the pioneers of volcanology in France.

As for Cap Faujas, now Point Hillier, its steep and rugged profile may likewise have suggested a volcanic landform. This appearance may have influenced Freycinet in his choice of the name. The resemblance, however, is purely one of landform: the cape is not of volcanic origin, but forms part of the region's ancient geological basement, composed mainly of granitic and metamorphic rocks.

In 1793, Faujas was appointed to the first chair of geology at the Muséum national d'Histoire naturelle in Paris. His writings and teaching thus reflect the transition from a largely descriptive science rooted in mineralogy towards a field-based geology seeking to reconstruct the physical history of the Earth through the study of landforms, geological strata, rocks and the processes that had shaped them.

Like many scientists of his time, Faujas was also involved in the political and cultural transformations of the Revolutionary period. In 1794–1795, following the advance of the French armies into the Low Countries, he took part, alongside the botanist André Thouin, in a mission charged with inventorying and seizing scientific and artistic collections in occupied territories. Natural history collections were thus transferred to Paris, contributing to the enrichment of French scientific institutions.
