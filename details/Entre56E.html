Archipel de la Recherche - Archipelago of La Recherche

Cap des Basses, Baie de l’Espérance, Cap Arride, La Chaussée, Île de Remarque, L’avant-garde, Île du Mondrain, Groupe de l'Ouest, Île du Milieu, Îles du Sud-Est, Groupe de l'Est, Baie de l'Espérance, Île de l'Observatoire, Cap Le Grand

The d’Entrecasteaux expedition surveyed the Archipelago of the Recherche in December 1792, during one of the most difficult stages of its exploration of the southern coast of New Holland. Despite the summer season, La Recherche and L’Espérance encountered strong winds, heavy seas and, above all, the constant presence of islands, reefs and shoals that were often difficult to distinguish. These navigational hazards were compounded by the urgent need to find fresh water.

The names bestowed on this stretch of coast reflect both the practical requirements of hydrographic surveying and the conventions of maritime voyages of discovery. Several describe physical features or the relative position of islands and thus provided useful landmarks for navigation: Îles du Sud-Est, Cap Aride, Île du Milieu, Groupe de l’Est, Île du Mondrain, Groupe de l’Ouest, La Chaussée, Cap des Basses and L’Avant-garde. Other place names preserve the memory of the expedition and the circumstances of the survey. Archipel de la Recherche and Baie de l’Espérance commemorate the expedition’s two ships, while Île de l’Observatoire recalls the astronomical and hydrographic observations carried out there. Cap Le Grand and Île Remarque are associated with the episode in which a member of the crew noticed a break in the surf, an observation that helped L’Espérance, then in a precarious position, reach a safer anchorage.

Geographical reconnaissance was accompanied by extensive natural-history work. Jacques-Julien Houtou de Labillardière made several landings within the archipelago, on Île de l’Observatoire and along the adjacent mainland coast. There he collected several plants then unknown to European science, including Chorizema ilicifolium, Anigozanthos rufus, Banksia repens and Banksia nivea. His observations also extended to terrestrial and marine fauna. In the waters of the archipelago, he notably recorded “enormous sharks of the most common species, Squalus carcharias”, some of which reached four metres in length. As was customary on natural-history expeditions of the period, birds and other animals were also captured or shot to provide specimens for scientific study.

Labillardière’s account also offers a glimpse of the division of labour, and at times the tensions, between naturalists and hydrographers. He relates how Charles-François Beautemps-Beaupré, the geographical engineer aboard La Recherche, set out at daybreak to survey the islets, search for a passage through the reefs and find a suitable source of fresh water. Labillardière regretted not being included in this reconnaissance, remarking with some bitterness that “geographical operations have little connection with the researches of naturalists”. He nevertheless acknowledged the scale of the work accomplished: Beautemps-Beaupré had “fixed the position of more than twenty islets” scattered over an area extending approximately one degree in both longitude and latitude.

The archipelago thus provides a particularly revealing example of the different dimensions of a late eighteenth-century maritime voyage of discovery. Navigation among reefs, the search for anchorages and fresh water, astronomical determination of position, hydrographic surveying, natural-history collecting and geographical naming all formed part of the same process of surveying and representing the territory. Beautemps-Beaupré’s surveys produced a remarkably detailed representation of this complex coastline, the quality of which was later acknowledged by Matthew Flinders when he explored this part of the Australian coast.
