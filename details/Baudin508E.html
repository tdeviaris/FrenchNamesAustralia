Île Bedout - Bedout Island

Cap Bedout - Cape Bedout

In a letter addressed to Vice-Admiral Bruix in 1798, quoted in the volume edited by Charles Duplomb, Récits maritimes ou Lettres inédites de marins français, accompagnées de commentaires (1924), Jacques Bédout forcefully expressed his dissatisfaction and sense of injustice. Although he had just been promoted to rear-admiral and appointed to command a squadron, the command was ultimately entrusted to another officer. He even referred to the “disgust” that this decision inspired in him. This personal testimony sheds light on the difficulties faced by the French Navy after the Revolution: frequent changes of command, the influence of political considerations on careers, and uncertainty surrounding appointments weakened the institution and fuelled rivalries among officers.

Bédout submitted his resignation in response to what he regarded as an affront, but it was not accepted. In 1799, he served aboard the Républicain and commanded the second division of the fleet under Admiral Eustache Bruix. After leaving Brest in April, the fleet sailed to Toulon and then to Genoa to support and supply the French army operating in Italy. In November, Bédout took command of a squadron that sailed from Lorient to Rochefort with the task of strengthening the coastal defences against a possible British attack.

In October 1802, Bonaparte entrusted him with the command of a new five-ship squadron, with the Argonaute as his flagship. Sailing from Brest in January 1803, it proceeded to Genoa, where it embarked approximately 5,000 Polish troops bound for Saint-Domingue, present-day Haiti. These soldiers were intended to reinforce the French expedition sent to the colony in an attempt to restore French authority as the Haitian Revolution entered its decisive phase. Marked by particularly violent fighting, disease, and the restoration of slavery in France’s other colonies, the campaign ultimately ended with the independence of Haiti in 1804.
