An author of the influential Traité d’astronomie, Lalande was also interested in the practical applications of astronomy to navigation. In 1793, he published an Abrégé de navigation, historique, théorique et pratique, accompanied by tables designed to determine true solar time from the observed altitude of the Sun or stars. These tables, which facilitated astronomical calculations at sea, were largely computed by his collaborator Marie-Jeanne Harlay, wife of Michel Lefrançois de Lalande, whom he referred to as his niece and who was also known by the name Amélie.

Lalande also oversaw the publication of the Histoire céleste française in 1801. Based on observations made by several astronomers, including Michel and Marie-Jeanne Lefrançois de Lalande, this work brought together data on nearly 50,000 stars. It was one of the most extensive undertakings in stellar observation and cataloguing of its time. These observations were subsequently used to compile a catalogue of 47,390 stars, published in 1847 after the necessary astronomical reductions had been carried out.

Lalande also sought to make astronomy accessible to a wider audience, particularly women. In his work published in 1786 as part of the Bibliothèque universelle des dames, he explained that he wished to present celestial phenomena without the mathematical complexities which, he believed, might discourage his female readers: “Our aim is to attract people to science, not to frighten them away at the outset.” This approach reflected a desire to disseminate scientific knowledge, while remaining influenced by the educational conventions of his time. It also contrasts with the important role he gave to female collaborators in his own research, notably Louise du Pierry and Marie-Jeanne Harlay, whose mathematical and astronomical expertise was recognised and comparable to that of their male counterparts.

Lalande’s work also contributed to the cartography of Baudin’s expedition. In the volume Navigation et géographie, Freycinet stated: “Messrs Lalande communicated to me the results of observations made at Greenwich by Dr Maskelyne.” These data, together with other observations made in Paris and Seeberg, were used in the astronomical calculations and checks necessary for preparing the expedition’s charts.

Lalande’s career thus illustrates the many ways in which astronomy contributed to the great voyages of maritime exploration. Through the publication of tables, the compilation of star catalogues, teaching, and the exchange of observations among European scientists, he helped advance the knowledge and methods that enabled navigators to determine their position at sea with greater accuracy. His work also demonstrates the importance of collective scientific endeavour, involving astronomers and female computers whose contributions often remained less visible than those of the scientists who directed and published the research.
