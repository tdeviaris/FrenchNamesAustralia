Pointe Jurien - White Rock Point

Jurien’s sea journal shows how the two frigates coordinated their observations and compared their results in order to improve the determination of their position. In the “Events and Remarks” column of his table, Jurien noted for 1-2 May 1792:

“At 5 o’clock, we communicated our longitude to the General, who immediately signalled his own to us as 32°59′.”

This exchange illustrates how observations were shared between the Espérance and La Recherche. Jurien’s nautical tables also record the elements required for dead reckoning: the ship’s course, speed, winds and drift, including that caused by currents. These data made it possible to estimate the vessel’s movement between observed positions. They were, however, only one part of the methods used by the expedition to determine longitude, which also relied on marine watches and astronomical observations. The values entered in Jurien’s tables are difficult to interpret directly today and need to be understood in relation to the calculating conventions and references used by navigators at the time.

In his Souvenirs, Jurien later recalled his participation in the reconnaissance of the North River, now the Derwent, and its neighbouring bays. He remembered that boats commanded by Saint-Aignan, lieutenant aboard La Recherche, and La Seinie, lieutenant aboard the Espérance, were sent to explore “the various branches of the channel”, and that he was allowed to take part in these reconnaissances.

He described an inlet extending far to the north-west, where soundings consistently gave depths of ten to twelve fathoms. The coast appeared wooded, but broken by clearings that seemed to offer easy access inland. As the men looked for a place to bring their boat ashore at the end of the day, they encountered a particularly striking rock formation: a very smooth stretch of shoreline, rising only a few inches above sea level, which Jurien compared to “an immense quay”. At the foot of this “quay”, they found nine fathoms of water over a muddy bottom. Jurien believed that “the largest ships could have moored there or been careened without the slightest danger”.

Decades later, when looking back on the expedition in his memoirs, Jurien expressed great pride in the scale of the work accomplished in Van Diemen’s Land. He believed that the French surveys had revealed to Europe a region remarkable for the intricate form of its coastline, the spaciousness of its roadsteads and the ease of access to its harbours.

His retrospective account was nevertheless tinged with bitterness. Referring to the later development of Hobart Town and the Derwent, he observed that the French predictions about the future of the region had indeed been fulfilled, but under British rule. He concluded that this “magnificent country” had prospered “not, as we had a right to hope, for the benefit of France”.
