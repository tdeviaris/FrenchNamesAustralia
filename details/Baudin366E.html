Baie Riedlé - Riedle Bay

The letters Anselme Riedlé wrote to André Thouin before the expedition's departure reveal both his enthusiasm for the mission and the practical preparations required for his work. Seeds, plants, boxes, tools and personal belongings had to be taken aboard, sometimes under difficult conditions. He particularly hoped to enrich the botanical collections of the Muséum national d'Histoire naturelle in Paris with plants collected during the voyage.

François Péron recounts that Riedlé's funeral, held at Kupang on 22 October 1801, was attended by the French crews and the Dutch authorities. A modest monument was erected over his grave, but Péron considered it especially meaningful because it was “consecrated by their shared grief”.

Remembering that David Nelson, botanist of the Bounty expedition, had died at Kupang in 1789, Péron, with Baudin's approval, set out to locate his grave. A Dutch soldier who had attended Nelson's burial showed him the site. Riedlé was thus buried beside his British predecessor, and the monument was dedicated to their joint memory. This gesture symbolically united two naturalists who had died during major maritime expeditions, transcending the rivalries between France and Britain.

Riedlé's memory also survives in botanical nomenclature. A cycad native to south-western Australia is now known as Macrozamia riedlei, formerly Cycas riedlei. A historical specimen collected by Théodore Leschenault de La Tour during the Baudin expedition, preserved in the herbarium of the Muséum national d'Histoire naturelle in Paris, bears the inscription “Nouvelle Hollande, Baye du Géographe” (New Holland, Geographe Bay).

Leschenault also paid tribute to his fellow expedition member in his botanical notebooks by giving the name Riedelera glandifera to a plant observed on Île des Amiraux, now Depuch Island, off the north-western coast of Western Australia. He accompanied this dedication with the words “en signe de tristesse” (as an expression of sorrow), recalling Riedlé's death at Timor. This manuscript name reflects the naturalists' attachment to their deceased companion, although it is not currently recognised as an accepted botanical name.
