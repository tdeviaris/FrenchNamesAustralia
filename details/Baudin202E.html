Anse Decrès – Decres Bay

Although his freedom of action was restricted by Napoleon’s close control over naval affairs, Decrès remained loyal to the Emperor while sometimes expressing a highly critical view of his policies. In his Memoirs, written several decades after the events, the Duke of Marmont recalled that in 1809, shortly after his elevation to the dignity of Marshal of the Empire, Decrès allegedly told him:

“Well, Marmont, you are very pleased because you have just been made a marshal. You see everything in the brightest light. Shall I tell you the truth and reveal the future to you? The Emperor is mad, completely mad, and he will throw every one of us head over heels, and all this will end in a terrible catastrophe.”

This retrospective account should be treated with caution, but it illustrates the forthrightness that Marmont attributed to the minister, as well as the concerns Decrès is said to have felt about the direction of the imperial regime.

In his journal, Baudin referred to the island as Île Borda, in honour of the naval officer, mathematician and scientist Jean-Charles de Borda—see Piton Borda on the Eyre Peninsula.

In February 1803, at King George Sound, Baudin encountered the Union, an American brig commanded by the captain and sealing master Isaac Pendleton. He provided Pendleton with information about the anchorages surveyed around the island and asked him, should he call there, to preserve the pigs and poultry that the expedition had left for the benefit of future navigators. He also directed him to an area where seals were relatively abundant.

Pendleton and his crew subsequently spent several months on the island during the austral winter of 1803. They established themselves in a bay which they named Union Harbour, now known as American River.
