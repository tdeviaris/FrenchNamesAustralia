Caroline Bonaparte: An Ambitious Queen in Naples

Caroline Bonaparte (1782–1839), Napoleon’s youngest sister, became Queen of Naples in 1808 alongside her husband, Marshal Joachim Murat. Ambitious, cultured and politically astute, she did not confine herself to the ceremonial role traditionally expected of a queen. She took part in governing the kingdom and served several times as regent during Murat’s absences, while having to contend with both her husband’s authority and that of Napoleon, who was determined to keep Naples within the French imperial system.

On her arrival in Naples in September 1808, Caroline reportedly received an enthusiastic welcome. According to the Neapolitan historian Pietro Colletta, the population admired her beauty, regal bearing and four young children. However, she never seems to have enjoyed the same personal popularity as her husband, Joachim Murat. Closely involved in government affairs, she sought in particular to secure the support of the Neapolitan aristocracy, sometimes at the expense of reforms introduced by the regime.

Caroline took a particular interest in developing the kingdom’s manufacturing industries. She encouraged silk and cotton production, as well as coral craftsmanship, a speciality of the Naples region. She also supported girls’ education. These initiatives reflected her reforming ambitions, although their results were limited by the kingdom’s financial difficulties, warfare and disruptions to maritime trade.

Her influence was particularly evident at Pompeii. On 27 October 1808, while visiting the site with Michele Arditi, who oversaw the excavations, she took part in the discovery of ancient objects. Enthusiastic about the experience, she encouraged the expansion of the excavations and provided funds from the royal household. At one point, as many as six hundred workers were employed simultaneously in uncovering the remains. In 1812, she proposed naming the districts and streets of Pompeii and numbering its houses, so that the precise locations of archaeological discoveries could be recorded. She also suggested publishing a monthly journal reporting on the excavations. The numbering of buildings was not introduced until several decades later, under the direction of Giuseppe Fiorelli. Caroline was therefore interested not merely in financing archaeological research, but also in improving its organisation and documentation.

Her interest in the arts was reflected in the decoration of the royal palaces and the commissions she gave to contemporary artists. She was particularly supportive of Ingres, from whom she commissioned La Grande Odalisque in 1814, now in the Louvre Museum. This celebrated painting illustrates the artistic ambitions of the Neapolitan court and the prestige Caroline sought to establish.

However, the political aspirations of Murat and Caroline increasingly conflicted with those of Napoleon. Seeking greater independence, the royal couple gradually attempted to free themselves from imperial control. Following the military defeats of 1812 and 1813, they turned towards Austria in the hope of preserving their kingdom. This strategy temporarily enabled Murat to retain his throne, but failed to prevent the collapse of the Neapolitan monarchy in 1815.

Caroline’s reign thus presents a mixed record. She helped stimulate certain economic activities, supported education and, above all, advanced archaeological research. Yet the pressures of war and the political ambitions of the royal couple ultimately undermined these efforts. Her career illustrates both the genuine authority exercised by some women of the imperial family and the limits of their independence in Napoleonic Europe.
