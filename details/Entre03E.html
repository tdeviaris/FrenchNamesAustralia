Recherche Bay - Baie de la Recherche - Laylatiya

Recherche Bay was the first Australian bay that the expedition “discovered” and, more generally, the first “discovery” of the voyage. The bay was visited twice: first from 21 April to 16 May 1792, and then from 21 January to 13 February 1793.

Naming what appeared to be an important discovery after the first ship to visit it was standard European practice. In 1773, for example, the British captain Tobias Furneaux named a bay on nearby Bruny Island after his ship, the Adventure. It is significant that, in this first coastal sketch, Beautemps-Beaupré designated the two ports of the bay as A and B, since the commander held the authority to name places.

The vessel la Recherche, formerly la Truite (the Trout), was built in 1787 and was used mainly for transporting wood for construction. It was approximately 36 metres in length and 9.10 metres wide, with a draught of less than 2.5 metres. The alterations made during the ship's fitting-out included the construction of an orlop deck, the clearing of part of the gundeck for cannons, the addition of a poop cabin for the captain's use on the after quarterdeck, and the doubling and nailing of the hull. The main innovation was in the galleys, which were built of iron instead of brick and designed to burn coal rather than wood. The crew was composed of one hundred and thirteen people at the time of the expedition's departure and was captained by Antoine Raymond Joseph de Bruni d'Entrecasteaux.

D'Entrecasteaux considered Recherche Bay a haven which was “one of the most practical and the safest”. He highlighted that it:

“contrasts strongly with the name given to the bay next to, but hidden from it [Storm Bay, the name given to it in 1642 by the Dutch navigator Abel Tasman]”, pointing out that “the water is so calm that the most violent winds barely disturb its surface; the frailest craft could sail on it at any time… The plans drawn by M. Beautemps-Beaupré, the expedition's chief engineer, assisted by the officers and M. Jouvency, second engineer, will provide details of this port and of the bay, at the end of which it is situated.”

During the first stop in 1792, the scholars carried out their activities along the shores: three tents were set up for astronomical observations, while the naturalists collected botanical specimens. During the second stop in 1793, the naturalists met with Aboriginal inhabitants in encounters described as friendly and even very cordial.

The principal difficulty reported by d'Entrecasteaux and his officers was the muddy sea bottom, into which the anchors sank and which made them difficult to haul in.

Further information: the Archipel de la Recherche in Western Australia was also named after d'Entrecasteaux's vessel.
