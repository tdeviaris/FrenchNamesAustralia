Île aux Perdrix - Partridge Island

The history of Île aux Perdrix provides a particularly concrete example of how place-name nomenclature was created during a maritime expedition. Names could arise from the very first boat reconnaissances, inspired by a feature of the landscape, an event or, as in this case, an observation of wildlife. Their adoption into the expedition's nomenclature, however, was the responsibility of the commander, who selected or formally endorsed names proposed by officers and members of reconnaissance parties.

During the reconnaissance led by Crétin, which set out on 30 April 1792, the men landed on the island and flushed “two coveys of partridges which appeared to us to be of the same species as those in Europe”. This initial identification gave rise to the name Île aux Perdrix, which d'Entrecasteaux formally endorsed by incorporating it into the expedition's nomenclature. Crétin also observed, some distance from the shore, three abandoned huts. From this he inferred that the inhabitants of the country probably came to stay on the island at certain seasons.

When Labillardière and Riche landed on the island in turn a few weeks later, their perspective as naturalists led to a different identification: they found numerous quails, but no partridges. Labillardière nevertheless did not reach a definite conclusion. He left open both the possibility that the first visitors had confused the two birds and the possibility that the partridges had left the island between the two visits.

This divergence is revealing of the way a scientific expedition operated. Officers reconnoitring the coasts had to describe places quickly and could propose names by which they might be identified; the commander selected or endorsed them, while the naturalists approached the same places with different methods and different criteria of classification. A place name could therefore become established even while the scientific knowledge underlying it remained provisional or open to question.

In the case of Île aux Perdrix, that first observation proved remarkably enduring. The name entered the expedition's cartography, and its meaning was later directly preserved in the modern English place name Partridge Island. The map thus retains the trace not only of a place surveyed in 1792, but also of a zoological identification that the expedition's naturalists had almost immediately called into question.
