Île Corvisart - Corvisart Island (Western Australia)

Baie Corvisart - Corvisart Bay (South Australia)

Cap Hallé - Cape Radstock

Jean-Noël Hallé (1754-1822) and Jean-Nicolas Corvisart-Desmarets (1755-1821) belonged to the same generation of physicians. Trained in Parisian hospital circles, they became colleagues at the new Paris School of Health, established during the Revolution, and together contributed to the renewal of medical teaching and practice. Their relationship was both professional and personal: the sources describe them as close colleagues involved in several of the same scientific and medical institutions. Hallé notably stood in for Corvisart in his teaching at the Collège de France when the latter became increasingly occupied with his duties to Napoleon.

Their principal fields of interest nevertheless differed. Corvisart devoted himself chiefly to clinical medicine and the diagnosis of internal diseases, particularly disorders of the heart and chest. As Napoleon’s First Physician, he held a central position within the medical service of the imperial court. Hallé, Professor of Medical Physics and Hygiene, was more broadly concerned with the relationships between health, climate and living conditions. He also attended several members of the imperial family, although he did not hold a position beside Napoleon equivalent to that of Corvisart.

Hallé promoted a particularly broad conception of hygiene. In his view, health depended not only on diet and exercise, but also on the quality of air and water, climate, housing, clothing, occupations and the material organisation of everyday life. His teaching thus sought to apply the physical sciences to the understanding of the human body and the prevention of disease.

This approach influenced François Péron, who had attended Hallé’s lectures before departing with the Baudin expedition. Aboard Le Géographe, Péron regularly recorded temperature, atmospheric pressure and humidity in different parts of the ship. He compared the air in the holds, between-decks spaces and other enclosed areas with that on deck in order to investigate possible relationships between atmospheric conditions and the health of the crew.

Péron partly attributed the unhealthy condition of the ship to heat, humidity and the vapours that accumulated in its confined spaces. He believed that washing the hold with seawater maintained a cold and damp atmosphere that was harmful to the crew’s health and liable to encourage the development of scurvy. This interpretation was incorrect: scurvy is caused by vitamin C deficiency. His observations nevertheless represented a methodical attempt to apply Hallé’s environmental and hygienic teaching to naval medicine. In his narrative, Péron accordingly reproached Baudin in severe terms for having neglected the sanitary instructions issued before departure, whose general spirit was consistent with the principles promoted by Hallé: “Finally, our own misfortunes may furnish a great and terrible lesson to navigators: these misfortunes, in fact, had no other cause than the contempt of our chief for all the precautions most essential to the health of the men […]”

This judgement, expressed after Baudin’s death and against a background of intense personal conflict, must be treated with caution. The commander’s journal shows that he himself took various hygienic measures. At King George Sound on 18 February 1803, he ordered one tent to be erected for the sick and another for the naturalists, and arranged for a reservoir to be prepared for washing the crew’s linen and hammocks. These measures did not prevent illness, but they qualify the image of a commander wholly indifferent to the health of his men.

Hallé valued his former student’s observations on naval hygiene and encouraged him to publish them. He also supported Péron’s admission to the Society of the Paris School of Medicine. The names Hallé and Corvisart on the French charts therefore referred to two colleagues with whom Péron’s scientific work had direct connections. These links extended into another major field of preventive medicine at the time: the campaign against smallpox.

Hallé and Corvisart also helped promote la vaccine in France, that is, vaccination intended to protect the population against smallpox. Both belonged to the first groups of physicians charged with examining Jenner’s method, verifying its effects and encouraging its adoption. They later served on the Comité central de vaccine, organised under the Consulate and further developed during the Empire. Alongside physicians such as Pinel, Guillotin and Husson, and with La Rochefoucauld-Liancourt (see Liancourt Point), they helped establish a network comprising departmental committees, repositories of vaccine material, medical vaccinators and a system for collecting results.

Through his observations, Péron also contributed to this programme. During Le Géographe’s stay at the Isle de France, now Mauritius, he investigated the spread of vaccination in the colony. He discussed the subject with the physician Léonard-Clair Laborde, who was attempting to preserve and propagate vaccine material. At a time when this material often had to be transferred directly from one recently vaccinated person to another, maintaining an uninterrupted chain of vaccination posed a major practical challenge.

Péron also witnessed the early introduction of vaccination at the Cape of Good Hope. After returning to France, he communicated his information to physicians involved in the campaign against smallpox. His observations gathered during the voyage thus corresponded directly with Hallé and Corvisart’s concerns: tracing the circulation of disease, examining local practices and contributing to the spread of a new preventive method.

The place names Cape Hallé, Corvisart Bay and Corvisart Island therefore commemorate more than two prominent physicians of the Napoleonic period. They associate the cartography of the voyage with two colleagues whose careers intersected in medical teaching, court service, scientific institutions and the campaign against smallpox. They also recall the links between Péron’s research, Hallé’s environmental conception of hygiene and the public-health work undertaken collectively by French physicians.
