Port du Sud - Rocky Bay

On 20 April 1792, the southern part of Recherche Bay was surveyed by the d’Entrecasteaux expedition. It offered a sheltered anchorage which the French named Port du Sud, the first Australian harbour surveyed and charted by the expedition. Its relatively calm waters, abundant and reliable supplies of fresh water, and the fishing opportunities reported by the officers brought welcome relief after several weeks of difficult navigation. Persistent westerly winds and heavy seas had severely tested the frigates, breaking chains and causing seawater to leak into the holds. D’Entrecasteaux himself had suffered a serious injury to his ribs after being violently thrown against the deck by the ship’s rolling. Port du Sud therefore provided an essential refuge for both the vessels and their crews.

When the expedition returned to Recherche Bay in early 1793, the harbour also acquired particular importance as a site for scientific observation. The astronomers established two observatories on its southern shore, one for La Recherche and the other for L’Espérance, from which they carried out their measurements.

D’Entrecasteaux paid particular attention to the tides, whose behaviour differed noticeably from that observed during the previous stay. Despite their irregularities, he noted that the sea rose and fell once every twenty-four hours and that, even at new and full moon, the rise did not exceed about four to five feet.

The astronomers also studied magnetic declination. Measurements taken at the observatories of La Recherche and L’Espérance, although the two sites were separated by only a few toises, produced noticeably different results. After ruling out instrumental error, d’Entrecasteaux considered that the nature of the ground might have exerted a local influence on the compasses. This observation illustrates the difficulties faced by the expedition when attempting to obtain comparable and reproducible measurements under field conditions.

Astronomical observations also made it possible to determine the latitude of Port du Sud at approximately 43° 34′ 30″ south. Longitude proved more difficult to establish precisely, as frequently unfavourable weather prevented several observations of stars and eclipses required for its calculation.

The atlas chart also shows the material organisation of the expedition’s stay: the fresh-water supply point on the western shore, three tents used for work and equipment, and the observatory established in 1793 on the south-eastern shore.
