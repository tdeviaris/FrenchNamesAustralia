About the image
Within the extensive grounds of the Carthusian monastery in Paris — three monks are shown on the right of the illustration — Le Monnier arranged two long metal wires to form a circuit measuring approximately 950 toises, or nearly 1.9 kilometres. A Leyden jar was placed at one end of the apparatus. When it was discharged, an observer positioned at the other end felt the electrical shock almost immediately.

The instruments available at the time could not measure such a brief interval precisely. Le Monnier therefore concluded that the propagation of the “electric matter” appeared virtually instantaneous and that its speed was at least thirty times greater than that of sound. Above all, the experiment demonstrated that an electrical discharge could be transmitted over a considerable distance through a conducting circuit.

Le Monnier’s scientific interests extended well beyond medical practice. His experiments helped demonstrate that electricity could be transmitted through conductors, including long metal wires and water. He also investigated atmospheric electricity and observed that electrical phenomena could be detected in the air not only during thunderstorms but also in fair weather. In addition, he wrote several articles for the Encyclopédie, notably on electricity, magnetism, magnets and the magnetic needle.

Le Monnier also possessed extensive knowledge of botany and medicinal plants. Appointed Professor of Botany at the Jardin du Roi in 1759, he contributed to the development of Louis XV’s plant collections at Trianon. He recommended Bernard de Jussieu — uncle of Antoine-Laurent de Jussieu — to the king, who entrusted him with the care and organisation of the gardens at Trianon.

Through his influence at court, at the Académie and at the Jardin du Roi, Le Monnier supported the travels and work of several botanists and plant collectors whose names also appear in the nomenclature of Péron and Freycinet.

René-Louiche Desfontaines was among his protégés. Le Monnier supported his botanical journey to North Africa, through territories corresponding to present-day Algeria and Tunisia, as well as the Atlas Mountains. Desfontaines regularly sent him accounts of his observations and succeeded him as Professor of Botany at the Jardin du Roi in 1786.

Jacques-Julien de La Billardière, who had first studied botany under Antoine Gouan at Montpellier, also benefited from Le Monnier’s patronage after arriving in Paris. Le Monnier supported his work and his journey to the Levant, during which La Billardière explored Lebanon and collected numerous botanical specimens.

Le Monnier also benefited from plants and seeds brought back or transmitted through a broad network of travellers, missionaries and colonial administrators. Pierre Poivre, who was active in the Indian Ocean and Asia, formed part of this wider movement to introduce exotic plants, although he was not Le Monnier’s pupil. Le Monnier attempted to acclimatise some of the species gathered abroad, concentrating on forest trees, fruit trees and plants considered medically or economically useful. Cuvier noted that he devoted several decades to these experiments in acclimatisation.
