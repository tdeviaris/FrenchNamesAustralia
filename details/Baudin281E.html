Cap Rosily -

Île Rosily - Rosily Islands, Rosily shoal

Transcription of the letter:

Dépôt général de la marine
Liberté Égalité
Paris le 26 prairial an 8e de la République française une et indivisible.
Le Vice-amiral Directeur et Inspecteur du Dépôt général de la Marine et des Colonies au Citoyen Berthoud
à Groslay
Je vous préviens, Citoyen, que je viens de recevoir l’ordre du ministre pour vous faire remettre six horloges Marines pour être réparées. Il me fait savoir que deux sont destinées pour le voyage autour du monde par le Capitaine Baudin et sont extrêmement pressées. Indiquez-moi par votre réponse si je dois vous les envoyer à la campagne ou si vous les ferez prendre au dépôt.
Salut et Considération
Signé Rosilly

This letter from Rosily to Berthoud — his signature here appears in the form “Rosilly” — is dated 26 Prairial Year VIII, or 15 June 1800. It provides direct evidence of the involvement of the director of the Dépôt général de la Marine in the material preparation of Baudin’s expedition. Rosily informed Ferdinand Berthoud that six marine timekeepers were to be sent to him for repair and specified that two of them were intended for “the voyage around the world by Captain Baudin” and therefore required urgent attention.

The expedition carried four marine chronometers by Louis Berthoud, Nos. 27, 31, 35 and 38, divided between the two vessels: Nos. 31 and 38 aboard the Géographe, and Nos. 27 and 35 aboard the Naturaliste. These instruments were intended to assist or check determinations of longitude during the voyage. Chronometer No. 35, constructed in 1798 and acquired by the Ministry of Marine in 1799, is particularly well documented. Rosily’s letter of 15 June 1800, however, does not give the numbers of the two timekeepers that he considered in urgent need of repair before departure, so they cannot be identified with certainty from this document alone.

The document therefore shows that Rosily’s role extended beyond the preservation and production of charts and hydrographic records. As head of the Dépôt, he was also involved in preparing the instruments required for the expedition’s astronomical observations and cartographic work. The letter thus establishes a direct documentary link between Rosily, the Berthoud horologists and the preparation of the voyage to the Southern Lands.

Rosily’s career, however, had begun much earlier in voyages of exploration and hydrographic reconnaissance. In 1772, he took part in Kerguelen’s first expedition to the Indian Ocean, initially aboard La Fortune. During the reconnaissance of the lands later known as the Kerguelen Islands, he participated in coastal operations before continuing the voyage aboard Le Gros Ventre after the two ships became separated.

From the mid-1780s, Rosily undertook a series of prolonged political, commercial and hydrographic missions. He sailed in the Mediterranean, the Red Sea, the Indian Ocean and the seas of China. In 1787, while commanding the Vénus, he carried out surveys in Egypt and the Gulf of Suez; observations made during this mission were subsequently used in the preparation of hydrographic charts of the area. His voyages also took him towards the Philippines and other Asian waters, where he gathered information on maritime routes and navigation conditions. These campaigns contributed substantially to his reputation as a hydrographer.

This experience helps explain his appointment on 22 August 1795 as director and inspector general of the Dépôt des cartes et plans de la Marine. From that position, Rosily occupied a central place in the organisation of French hydrography and nautical cartography.

In 1805, Napoleon appointed him to replace Villeneuve as commander of the combined Franco-Spanish fleet at Cadiz. Rosily arrived too late to prevent Villeneuve from sailing and suffering defeat at Trafalgar. He subsequently took command of the French ships that had remained or returned to Cadiz. In June 1808, at the beginning of the Spanish uprising, his squadron, caught between Spanish forces in the bay and the British blockade offshore, was forced to surrender. Rosily then returned to France and resumed his duties at the Dépôt de la Marine.

When Freycinet was preparing the charts and publications resulting from the Baudin expedition, Rosily therefore occupied a leading position within the institution responsible for centralising hydrographic records and producing French nautical charts. The two men necessarily belonged to the same professional environment; direct contact between them is highly likely, although a specific meeting should not be stated as fact without explicit documentary evidence.
