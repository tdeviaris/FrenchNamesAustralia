About the coastal profiles

Maurouard's hydrographic journal is preserved in the Marine series of the Archives nationales. It covers part of the voyage between July 1801 and March 1802 and brings together hydrographic observations, bearings and numerous coastal profiles. Maurouard himself described it as a “Journal containing all the work relating to the charts.”

On 17 and 18 February 1802, as the expedition navigated along south-eastern Van Diemen's Land, the profiles drawn by Maurouard formed part of a collective effort to survey and establish the position of the coastline. His drawings were not simply landscape views: combined with the bearings and angular measurements taken during navigation, they helped identify coastal landforms and contributed to plotting them on the charts.

Jean-Marie-Toussaint Maurouard was born in Caen in 1772. He began his naval career as a novice aboard L'Impétueux. Captured in May 1794 during naval operations commanded by Villaret-Joyeuse, he spent seventeen months as a prisoner before being admitted to the École polytechnique. There he received an advanced scientific education, particularly in mathematics, physics and geometry, which he subsequently complemented at the École des Ponts et Chaussées between 1797 and 1799 before returning to the Navy.

The expedition gave his name to two geographical features. Cape Maurouard, on the eastern coast of Maria Island, recalls his involvement in the geographical work undertaken in this part of Van Diemen's Land. Péron and Freycinet described him as “the estimable midshipman who at that time shared with M. Boullanger responsibility for the geographical work.” Farther north, Péron and Freycinet also gave his name to Île Maurouard, now St Helens Island. In this second instance, the account states that the island was named after “the midshipman who commanded our longboat on the occasion” being described. The two names therefore linked Maurouard directly with the hydrographic reconnaissance in which he took part.

Assessments of Maurouard by his commanders were nevertheless contrasting. Baudin, who had promoted him earlier in the voyage, later judged his conduct severely and transferred him to Le Naturaliste at Sydney in November 1802. Hamelin took a more favourable view and emphasized, in particular, his experience in commanding a watch. These differing assessments show that, within the expedition, recognition of scientific and technical abilities did not necessarily coincide with judgments of conduct or aptitude for command.

After returning to France in June 1803, Maurouard was promoted to ensign in October of the same year and to lieutenant in 1808. He remained almost continuously at sea until 1814 and received a master mariner's certificate in 1816. A Chevalier of the Légion d'honneur since 1811, he subsequently disappears from the presently known sources.
