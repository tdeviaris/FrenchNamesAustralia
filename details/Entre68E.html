Îles Saint-Alouarn - Saint Alouarn Islands

This French expedition was commanded by Yves-Joseph de Kerguelen de Trémarec (1734-1797), who was entrusted in 1771 by King Louis XVI to search for the unknown Southern Land. Kerguelen sailed in La Fortune, and his second-in-command, Louis-François de Saint-Aloüarn, commanded the gabarre Le Gros Ventre. The two ships became separated in the Indian Ocean. Kerguelen discovered a fog-shrouded island in the southern Indian Ocean and, believing it to be the unknown land, sailed back to France to report his discovery. The island group now bears his name. Following his instructions, Saint-Aloüarn continued east and made landfall on the western Australian coast near Cape Leeuwin on 17 March 1772.

During the seventeenth century the Dutch and the British explorer Dampier (1651-1715) had already visited this coast, then referred to as New Holland. Following the coast north, Saint-Aloüarn anchored at Dirk Hartog Island, where he named Pointe des Hauts-Fonds, the northernmost cape of the peninsula. He organized a ceremony to claim possession of the land and another to bury one of his crew, Massicot, who had likely died of scurvy. According to Sergeant Lafortune’s journal, as cited by Godard and de Kerros, Massicot’s name was given to the bay where he was buried; however, this place name was never adopted in the French atlases published by later voyages that visited western Australia. Saint-Aloüarn then sailed north as far as Melville Island. He and his second-in-command died a few days after their return to the Île de France. No action was taken by the French king to take possession of the land.

In 1998, a French écu of 1767 and a broken bottle from this expedition were discovered on a cliff top at Dirk Hartog Island, together with a second bottle containing another écu, partially sealed with a lead capsule. The first écu was found in January by an expedition led by Philippe Godard and including members of the Batavia Coast Maritime Heritage Group from Geraldton. In March-April 1998, in collaboration with the Centre for Archaeology at the University of Western Australia, the Western Australian Museum sent an archaeological expedition to the site, which uncovered the French wine bottle sealed with lead and containing another French écu dated 1767.
