Port du Nord - Pigsties Bay

The expedition anchored at Port du Nord, in the northern part of Recherche Bay. D'Entrecasteaux emphasised the advantages of this sheltered harbour, particularly for maintaining and repairing the ships:

“The harbour in which we stayed is the most suitable place for carrying out repairs that might be required as a result of the bad weather encountered in these stormy seas. The other large bays surveyed in the north-eastern part of Recherche Bay, although offering equally safe shelter, would be too extensive, and the sea too rough, to allow such operations to be undertaken. They are magnificent anchorages, situated close to an excellent harbour.”

In 1792, in order to determine the geographical coordinates of the anchorage accurately and check the performance of the marine chronometers, members of the crews of both ships established an astronomical observatory on the shore. The two observatories were located close to one another, facilitating communication, the exchange of information and operations associated with the maintenance of the vessels. Although astronomical observations were limited by weather conditions and the time available, they were supplemented and compared with those made the following year at Port du Sud, helping to refine the geographical positions of both harbours.

The Port du Nord Vegetable Garden: An Experiment in Plant Acclimatisation

As part of the expedition's natural history activities, the gardener Félix Lahaye (also known as Lahaie or Delahaye; see the entry for Île Lahaye) established a vegetable garden near the shore, “east-north-east of our anchorage”, according to d'Entrecasteaux. Its location was marked on the plan of Port du Nord. Labillardière explained:

“We had on board a great variety of European seeds, which it would be useful to propagate in this extremity of New Holland. The prevailing temperature led us to hope that they would thrive.”

The establishment of this garden was not simply a personal initiative. It formed part of a broader programme for the introduction and dissemination of useful plants, outlined in the instructions prepared for d'Entrecasteaux's expedition. By the late eighteenth century, major scientific voyages were concerned not only with collecting specimens for European institutions but also with transferring plants between continents, in the hope of acclimatising food crops and other economically valuable species to new environments.

The gardener's instructions called for fruit trees and useful plants to be established in parts of New Holland where they were likely to thrive, preferably near anchorages that might be visited by European ships. The introduced plants were also intended to benefit Indigenous populations, and the gardener was encouraged to explain how they could be cultivated and propagated. Yet this supposedly philanthropic ambition reflected European assumptions about agriculture and diet, in which the adoption of cultivated plants and European farming practices was often regarded as a form of progress, with little recognition of the environmental knowledge and land-management practices developed over thousands of years by Aboriginal peoples.

Lahaye sowed several varieties of vegetables brought from France in a plot of ground prepared with considerable difficulty. He also scattered seeds in more open areas of the surrounding forest, wherever the soil appeared suitable for germination.

When the expedition returned to the bay in January 1793, the garden had achieved only limited success. A few chicory plants, cabbages, sorrel, radishes, cress and potatoes had germinated, but their growth remained extremely limited. Lahaye attributed the poor results partly to drought, the hardness of the soil and the late planting of the seeds.

During a visit to the garden with an Aboriginal inhabitant, the latter immediately distinguished the young European seedlings from the native vegetation, although they were still barely visible. Lahaye also gathered a few very small potatoes and attempted to explain that they could be eaten after being cooked in the embers of a fire. This episode provides a particularly interesting record of an early attempt to communicate European horticultural practices to the local inhabitants, although there is no evidence that these practices were subsequently adopted.

Despite its modest results, the garden illustrates one of the ambitions characteristic of late eighteenth-century scientific voyages: not only to collect and study plants, but also to transport, acclimatise and disseminate them to other parts of the world.

Elsewhere in the area, the botanist Labillardière discovered an evergreen tree whose fruit resembled a cashew nut, which he described under the name Exocarpos cupressiformis. The expedition's artist, Piron, produced several views of the surrounding landscape, whose beauty impressed both the commander and the naturalists.

The Search for the Garden's Remains

In February 2003, a rectangular arrangement of stones, measuring approximately nine by seven metres and divided into four plots, was discovered on the north-eastern peninsula of Recherche Bay. The structure was initially interpreted as the possible remains of the vegetable garden established by Lahaye in 1792.

However, subsequent archaeological investigations failed to confirm this identification. Although the garden's position had been marked on the plan of Port du Nord published in the expedition's official atlas, its exact location remains uncertain. Archaeological studies suggest that the garden was more likely situated near a small stream, approximately 70–100 metres south-east of the stone structure discovered in 2003.
