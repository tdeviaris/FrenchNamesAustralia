Cap d’Orvillier - Cape St Albans

Etching engraved by Joseph Collyer and published by John Bew in London on 10 April 1779.

The print shows the centre of the battle and identifies several engagements between British and French warships by means of numbered references:

1. Formidable (90 guns) engaging La Couronne (80);
2. Robust (74) engaging Le Glorieux (74);
3. Vigilant (64) engaging Le Saint-Michel (64);
4. Prince George (90) engaging Le Vengeur (64);
5. Foudroyant (80) engaging L’Actionnaire (64);
6. Victory (100) engaging La Bretagne (110);
7. Bienfaisant (64) engaging La Ville de Paris (92).

The original drawing is described as having been made by an officer serving aboard Victory. The print was engraved by Joseph Collyer and published by the London bookseller John Bew, whose premises were at 29 Paternoster Row. The detailed identification of the vessels follows the inscriptions on the print and its associated catalogue description and should therefore be understood as a contemporary British representation of the engagement.

In 1778, Louis Guillouet, comte d’Orvilliers, was given command of France’s principal Atlantic fleet. On 27 July, he encountered Admiral Augustus Keppel’s British fleet off Ushant in the first major naval engagement between France and Great Britain following France’s formal entry into the American War of Independence.

The battle produced no decisive result: no ships of the line were captured or sunk, and the two fleets withdrew after sustaining casualties and material damage. D’Orvilliers nevertheless retained a tactical position that might have enabled him to renew the engagement against the temporarily disordered British line. The signals and orders sent to the French van were not, however, carried out as rapidly as he expected.

Responsibility for this episode was subsequently attributed to the duc de Chartres, the future Philippe Égalité, who was aboard the Saint-Esprit and nominally held a command in the van. This interpretation, widely circulated in contemporary and later accounts, should nevertheless be treated with caution: the effective command of the ship and the transmission of orders also involved several professional naval officers. The Battle of Ushant consequently gave rise to controversy in both navies, without allowing either side to claim an undisputed victory.

In 1779, d’Orvilliers took command of a large combined Franco-Spanish fleet assembled as part of a projected landing in Great Britain. The campaign was compromised by the premature departure and inadequate provisioning of the French fleet, the delayed arrival of the Spanish squadron at the designated rendezvous, difficulties in coordinating the two navies, incomplete intelligence and unfavourable winds. Disease also spread among crews confined aboard their ships during an exceptionally prolonged campaign.

D’Orvilliers’ only son, who was serving with the fleet, fell ill and died on 2 August 1779. Despite this loss, d’Orvilliers continued the operation for a time. The allied fleet entered the English Channel but was unable either to force a decisive engagement with the Royal Navy or to establish the conditions required for a landing. The expedition was finally abandoned in early September.

Deeply affected by his son’s death and by the failure of the campaign, d’Orvilliers asked to be relieved of his command. The episode effectively brought his operational career as a fleet commander to an end.
