Cap Olivier - Peerless Point

Guillaume-Antoine Olivier's (1756–1814) major work, Entomologie, ou Histoire naturelle des insectes, avec leurs caractères génériques et spécifiques, leur description, leur synonymie et leur figure enluminée, was published progressively between 1789 and 1808. Despite its general title, it was ultimately devoted to beetles (Coleoptera), an order of insects that includes scarabs, weevils and longhorn beetles. The complete work comprises six volumes of text and 363 engraved plates, collected in two additional volumes. These illustrations, sometimes hand-coloured, provided detailed representations of the shapes, proportions and distinguishing characteristics of individual species.

The work reflects advances in zoological classification during the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries. Olivier sought to distinguish genera and species through their morphological characteristics, compare descriptions published by different naturalists and establish their synonymy. Combining systematic descriptions with detailed illustrations, his Entomologie became an important reference work for identifying beetles from different parts of the world.

This scientific undertaking formed part of the same broader movement as the natural history investigations of the Baudin expedition (1800–1804). Organised with the involvement of the Muséum national d'histoire naturelle in Paris, the expedition was intended not only to survey and chart the Australian coastline but also to collect animals, plants and minerals, enriching French collections and extending knowledge of the natural world of the southern lands. The expedition's naturalists, including François Péron and René Maugé, took part in these collecting activities, while Charles-Alexandre Lesueur made a major contribution to illustrating the specimens observed. According to an inventory published in 1804, more than 4,000 insect specimens were brought back by the expedition.

Although Olivier did not participate in the voyage, the Australian collections could be studied using methods of description and classification similar to those he had helped develop. His work thus illustrates the complementary roles of voyages of exploration, museum collections and major illustrated scientific publications in the development of natural history during the early nineteenth century.
