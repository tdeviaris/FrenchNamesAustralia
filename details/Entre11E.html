Pointe Labillardière - Point Labillardiere

Labillardière joined d'Entrecasteaux's expedition with substantial experience as a naturalist and with well-established connections in European scientific circles. Trained as a physician, he soon chose to devote himself to natural history, and especially to botany. He spent time in Britain, where he studied cultivated plants and came into contact with Joseph Banks, before travelling through the Mediterranean and the Near East to build botanical collections. In Paris, he was a member of the Société d'Histoire naturelle, founded in 1790, which included several naturalists who would later take part in the expedition. The Society's published membership lists notably include Labillardière, Antoine Riche and Étienne-Pierre Ventenat.

His observations in Van Diemen's Land show how closely the voyage combined contemplation of the landscape, scientific collecting and the search for possible practical uses. In his Relation du voyage à la recherche de La Pérouse, he wrote of the forests:

“We were seized with admiration at the sight of these ancient forests which the axe had not yet degraded. The eye was astonished by the prodigious height of these trees…”

This was the perspective of a late-eighteenth-century European naturalist; it does not mean that these landscapes were without human history, since they had been occupied and shaped for thousands of years by Palawa peoples.

Labillardière did not merely identify plants. He also considered their usefulness. When he recognised Apium prostratum, he noted its resemblance to other species of the same genus and concluded that it could be eaten. A substantial quantity was taken back on board, where fresh greens were particularly welcome after a long passage sustained largely by preserved provisions. Labillardière's 1799 Relation is the primary source for this episode.

He adopted the same approach to the tree he later named Eucalyptus globulus. Labillardière was interested not only in its botanical characteristics, but also in its timber. He considered its trunk suitable for naval construction and even contemplated its use for masts. A nineteenth-century source reproducing the passage states:

“The trunk is suitable for naval construction and could be used for masts, although it is neither as light nor as elastic as pine.”

It also records that Labillardière had a tree felled in order to collect its flowers, and that eucalyptus timber was used for repairs to one of the expedition's boats.

These observations capture his working method particularly well: collecting, describing, comparing and naming, while also investigating the possible uses of the species encountered. After his return to France, his collections and notes provided material for several major works, notably his Relation du voyage à la recherche de La Pérouse and Novae Hollandiae plantarum specimen. The Bibliothèque nationale de France preserves the original 1799 edition of the Relation as well as its atlas.
