Cap Sévigné – Rapid Head

Orphaned at an early age and widowed at twenty-five, Madame de Sévigné enjoyed an unusual degree of independence for a woman of her rank in seventeenth-century France. She led an active social life and maintained a lifelong interest in reading, writing and conversation.

She moved in Parisian literary circles, notably those of the Hôtel de Rambouillet, famous for its “Blue Room”, and the Saturday gatherings hosted by Madeleine de Scudéry. She also maintained a close relationship with her relative and friend Madame de La Fayette, author of La Princesse de Clèves, published anonymously in 1678.

In conversation and correspondence alike, Madame de Sévigné was distinguished by her wit, keen observation and lively style. Her gift for storytelling, combining apparent spontaneity, vivid imagery and a subtle sense of irony, is particularly evident in her letters to her daughter.

In a letter dated 5 February 1674, the Marquise recounts with humour an accident involving the Archbishop of Reims's carriage. Racing through Nanterre, the carriage knocks down a rider and his horse before overturning itself. The man and his horse manage to get to their feet and escape, while the furious Archbishop demands that the rider be caught and punished:

“The Archbishop of Reims was returning from Saint-Germain yesterday at a tremendous pace, like a whirlwind. He thinks himself a very great lord, and his servants think him greater still. They were passing through Nanterre, at full speed, when they came upon a man on horseback. ‘Make way! Make way!’ they shouted. The poor man tried to move aside, but his horse would not budge. Then the carriage and its six horses came thundering down upon him, knocking both horse and rider to the ground, and passed right over them. So violent was the encounter that the carriage itself overturned.

Yet, miraculously, instead of being crushed and maimed, the man and his horse managed to get to their feet. The rider scrambled back into the saddle and fled, while the footmen, the coachman, and even the Archbishop himself began shouting, ‘Stop him! Stop the scoundrel! Give him a hundred blows!’

The Archbishop, recounting the incident, declared, ‘Had I caught that wretch, I would have broken his arms and cut off his ears.’”

“Farewell, my dearest and most beloved daughter. I cannot tell you how much I long to see you.”

The contrast between the rider's misfortune and the Archbishop's excessive anger gives the account much of its comic force. Without passing explicit judgement, Madame de Sévigné allows the prelate's own words to expose his arrogance and brutality.

Her letters to her daughter also reveal deep maternal affection, expressed within the social and emotional conventions of her milieu. They belong to a culture of feeling and conversation also reflected in the Carte de Tendre, the imaginary geography devised by Madeleine de Scudéry in her novel Clélie, histoire romaine, and associated with the ideals of galanterie.

Madame de Sévigné also commented on contemporary theatre, including Racine's plays, among them Bajazet, which continued to be performed during the Napoleonic period (see Cape and Isle Racine).

Several women and men of letters from the age of Louis XIV, belonging to the same literary and cultural world as Madame de Sévigné, were likewise commemorated in French geographical nomenclature. Taken together, these place-names reflect the importance attached to the literary heritage of the age of Louis XIV in Napoleonic French culture.

Further information. Baudin did not explicitly name the cape, but gave a nearby headland the name Pointe aux Corbeaux (“Crow Point”). The name Cape d'Aboukir appears at an earlier stage of French cartography, before Cape Sévigné was ultimately adopted by Péron. The present name, Rapid Head, derives from the brig Rapid, aboard which Colonel William Light arrived in South Australia in 1836.
