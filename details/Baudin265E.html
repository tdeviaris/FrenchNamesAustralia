Île Eugène – St Peter Island

Eugène de Beauharnais (1781–1824) was the son of Joséphine and Alexandre de Beauharnais, who was guillotined during the Terror. After his mother’s marriage to Napoleon Bonaparte, he accompanied his stepfather during the Italian and Egyptian campaigns and became one of his most loyal associates. When Napoleon became King of Italy in 1805, he appointed Eugène, then twenty-three years old, Viceroy of Italy. From Milan, Eugène administered the kingdom while remaining closely subject to imperial directives.

Eugène’s career illustrates Napoleon’s dynastic policy. In order to consolidate his power and integrate the Empire into the European monarchical system, Napoleon placed his brothers, sisters and close relatives at the head of kingdoms, or married them into established ruling houses.

Eugène occupied a distinctive position within this system. Napoleon formally adopted him on 12 January 1806 and designated him as a possible heir to the Italian crown after himself and his direct descendants, while excluding him from the succession to the French throne. Two days later, Eugène married Princess Auguste-Amélie, daughter of King Maximilian I of Bavaria, in Munich—see Cape Amélie. This union brought the new Napoleonic dynasty closer to the House of Wittelsbach, one of Europe’s oldest ruling families, and strengthened the alliance between France and Bavaria.

Although arranged for political reasons and concluded before the future spouses had truly come to know one another, the marriage became a lasting and apparently happy union. Eugène and Auguste-Amélie had seven children. Behind the prince, viceroy and military commander, Eugène appears as a man deeply attached to his wife, his children and his mother.

Eugène was profoundly affected by Joséphine’s divorce in 1809, which Napoleon had imposed. After the Emperor’s fall, he withdrew to his wife’s family in Bavaria, where he received the title Duke of Leuchtenberg. Through the marriages of his children, his descendants became durably integrated into Europe’s royal dynasties: his daughter Joséphine notably married the future King Oscar I of Sweden. Napoleon’s dynastic ambition thus survived, paradoxically, less through the thrones he had distributed than through the family alliances formed around Eugène and Auguste-Amélie.
