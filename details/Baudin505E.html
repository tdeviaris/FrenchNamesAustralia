In 1764, Pierre-Isaac Poissonnier (1720–1797) was appointed Inspector-General of Medicine, Surgery and Pharmacy for the Navy and the Colonies. A physician, teacher and administrator, he contributed to the organisation of naval medicine and published several works intended for the instruction of the Navy’s medical personnel. He remains particularly associated with his experiments in the desalination of seawater.

In the early 1760s, he introduced a small distillation apparatus designed to produce fresh drinking water aboard ship. Seawater was heated and the resulting vapour was condensed, producing water from which the salt had been removed. His priority in the invention nevertheless remains open to question, since Jean Gautier and other experimenters had already designed and tested comparable devices in the early eighteenth century.

Poissonnier’s originality therefore lay less in the principle of distillation than in its adaptation to the constraints of navigation. His apparatus was connected to the galley stove, used a water-cooled condensation system and was intended to be sufficiently compact and stable to operate despite the ship’s movements. It was principally for these practical features that it attracted the attention of the French naval administration.

An apparatus of this type was carried on Bougainville’s voyage around the world, undertaken by the Boudeuse and the Étoile between 1766 and 1769. The heat from the galley stove could be used simultaneously for preparing meals and distilling seawater. The process nevertheless had significant limitations: it consumed fuel, occupied valuable space and prolonged the use of fire in the galley of a wooden ship. It therefore appears to have served as an auxiliary device rather than as a means capable of supplying the entire daily water requirements of a crew.

The Baudin expedition did not carry Poissonnier’s apparatus. Its ships were instead fitted with a new type of galley designed by Citizen Garreau, whose installation was intended to improve certain uses of water and heat aboard ship. The innovation did not, however, give complete satisfaction. In a particularly critical assessment, the officer Ronsard (see Cape Ronsard) concluded that, when compared with the earlier arrangements, “everything is to the disadvantage of the innovation”.

Antoine-Marie Poissonnier Desperrières (1723–1793), Pierre-Isaac’s younger brother, worked alongside him in the medical administration of the hospitals of the Navy and the Colonies. He also spent several years in Saint-Domingue, where he observed the diseases affecting Europeans, travellers and seafarers in tropical climates.

In his works on maritime medicine, he argued that the health of ships’ crews depended not only on treatments administered after illness had developed, but also on diet, prevention, hygiene and the organisation of life aboard ship. In particular, he recommended reducing the excessive reliance on salted or smoked meat and bacon, while increasing the proportion of cereals, rice, dried pulses and foods that were easier to digest.

Following observations comparable to those made by the Scottish physician James Lind, Poissonnier Desperrières also emphasised the usefulness of acidic fruit juices, particularly lemon and orange juice, in preventing scurvy. He recommended thickening them in a bain-marie, preserving them in carefully stoppered bottles and distributing them regularly to ships’ crews.

Despite these recommendations, the medical knowledge transmitted to Péron and to the medical personnel of the Baudin expedition did not yet provide them with fully effective means of combating the principal diseases of long voyages, particularly scurvy and dysentery. The beneficial effects of certain foods were known empirically, but the physiological causes of these diseases remained poorly understood.

Baudin appears to have become more clearly aware of the effectiveness of lemon juice during his stay at Port Jackson. Before returning to sea at the end of 1802, he had seventy-two bottles taken aboard. Hamelin likewise recorded in his journal that he drank lemon juice and believed that he benefited from it, unlike much of his crew, who did not consume it regularly.

This medical context gives particular significance to the names Poissonnier Island and Percy Island on French charts. They belong to a commemorative geography in which the coastline of southern Australia served to honour not only navigators and administrators, but also men whose research and public service had contributed to improving the health and living conditions of seafarers.
