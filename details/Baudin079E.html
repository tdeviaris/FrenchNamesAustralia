Elisa Bonaparte: Governing under Napoleon’s Authority

Elisa Bonaparte (1777–1820), Napoleon’s younger sister, was one of the most active members of the imperial family. Princess of Piombino and Lucca from 1805, and Grand Duchess of Tuscany from 1809, she exercised genuine political and administrative authority, while her husband, Félix Baciocchi, remained largely excluded from government affairs. Educated at the royal school of Saint-Cyr, cultured and passionate about literature and the arts, she was determined to govern the territories entrusted to her.

In Lucca, she set about reforming the administration, developing education and reshaping the urban landscape. In 1807, she commissioned the creation of a large square in front of the ducal palace, today’s Piazza Napoleone. To make way for it, she did not hesitate to have several historic buildings demolished, including a sixteenth-century church. This ambitious project illustrates both her determination to modernise the city and the authoritarian nature of some of her decisions.

She also took a particular interest in girls’ education. In 1807, she founded the Elisa Institute in Lucca and sought to improve the organisation of teaching in religious institutions.

In the Carrara region, renowned for its marble quarries, she encouraged the development of both industry and artistic production. She supported the Academy of Fine Arts, promoted the training of sculptors and employed Lorenzo Bartolini, one of the leading artists of his time. A bank known as the Banque élisienne helped finance the marble industry, which supplied the material for sculptures celebrating Napoleon and his family.

Elisa personally supervised financial matters, selected her officials and took an interest in everything from manufacturing to mines, forests and saltworks. She had her decrees published in both French and Italian to accommodate local practices. Yet her independence remained strictly limited by Napoleon, who insisted on retaining control over essential decisions, particularly taxation and conscription. In 1809, he bluntly reminded her: “You are a subject, and like all French people, you are obliged to obey the orders of the ministers.”

An incident in 1811 illustrates these tensions. At the launching of the brig L’Inconstant, sailors reportedly shouted: “Long live Elisa! Long live the Emperor!” The order of these acclamations, reported in the press, irritated Napoleon. He considered it inappropriate that his sister should be mentioned before him and demanded stricter supervision of articles concerning her.

The fall of the Empire brought her rule to an end in 1814. Despite their disagreements, Napoleon later acknowledged his sister’s administrative abilities at Saint Helena, praising her intelligence, energy and thorough knowledge of government affairs. Elisa had demonstrated her ability to exercise genuine political power, while remaining subject to an imperial authority that allowed her only limited independence.
