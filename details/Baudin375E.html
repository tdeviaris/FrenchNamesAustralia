The Baudin expedition and the beginnings of field geology

At the turn of the nineteenth century, geology had not yet become an independent scientific discipline in the modern sense. It was developing out of mineralogy, the study of rock formations and strata, and broader questions concerning the formation and history of the Earth. Voyages of discovery offered an exceptional field for such research, enabling European naturalists to compare rocks, landforms and geological formations encountered in widely separated parts of the world.

The Baudin expedition gave significant attention to these investigations. Louis Depuch (see related place names) and Joseph-Charles Bailly were responsible for mineralogical and geological research. Bailly had studied at the École Polytechnique and was officially appointed in September 1800 as an élève minéralogiste (“student mineralogist”). After his return, he recalled that Depuch and he had been appointed as minéralogistes-géologues (“mineralogist-geologists”), and that their investigations were to follow the principles set out in earlier instructions by Déodat de Dolomieu, one of France’s leading authorities on rocks and geological formations.

Bailly described their programme as an attempt to determine as precisely as possible the materials composing the “beds” (bancs) and “strata” (couches) of the lands they visited, to collect different types of rocks and minerals and, whenever possible, to associate these specimens with accurate views of the coasts from which they came. Collecting was therefore not intended simply as an accumulation of natural curiosities. Specimens were meant to document the structure of the terrain and provide material for comparison after the expedition’s return to Europe.

This ambition, however, was constrained by the particular conditions of maritime exploration. Bailly himself gave a particularly clear account of these difficulties. In regions where Europeans had neither roads nor mines providing access to deeper geological formations, the naturalist often had to rely on whatever was exposed close to the landing place. During such voyages, he observed, a scientist might have only a few hours ashore and was therefore “forced to restrict himself to the natural productions presented to him by the seashore.”

The geology practised during the expedition was consequently, to a large extent, a geology of the coast. Cliffs, headlands, beaches, exposed rocks and landforms visible from the shore provided the principal sites of observation. The mineralogists collected specimens, described their appearance, and attempted to identify the arrangement of strata and the relationships between different types of rock. Landscapes themselves could provide geological clues. At Baie Fleurieu, for example, Bailly observed two ranges of high mountains enclosing the bay, giving it the appearance of a great valley invaded by the sea.

The collections brought back from the voyage were intended to extend these necessarily brief observations ashore. Bailly explained that they could provide geologists with comparative material concerning “the nature of the soil of these distant regions.” They included not only rocks and minerals but also fossil shells and other evidence that might contribute to understanding the history of geological formations. Bailly noted, in particular, marine fossil deposits found at some distance from the existing shoreline or at a certain elevation above it—observations that were significant in contemporary discussions about past transformations of the Earth’s surface.

The work of Bailly and Depuch thus illustrates an important stage in the development of the Earth sciences. Their approach still combined mineralogy, landscape description, specimen collecting and reflection on the formation of the globe. Yet it already relied on principles that would become fundamental to field geology: observing rocks in their geographical and geological context, comparing formations, describing their arrangement, and relating collected specimens to their location in the landscape.

The Baudin expedition was therefore part of a broader movement that, in the early nineteenth century, gradually transformed the study of minerals and rocks into a historical science of the Earth. Its geological results were necessarily incomplete, given the constraints under which the naturalists worked, but Bailly’s writings are particularly valuable because they document both the ambitions of this programme and the very practical limitations faced by some of the earliest geologists to undertake scientific investigations during long-distance voyages of exploration.
