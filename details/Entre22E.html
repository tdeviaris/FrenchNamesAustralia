Cap Riche - Cape Riche

Claude Antoine Gaspard Riche (1762-1797) was selected in 1791 by the Société d’Histoire naturelle de Paris to take part in the expedition. Like Labillardière, he had initially studied medicine before developing a strong interest in natural history. Serving as a naturalist aboard L’Espérance, he worked particularly in the fields of mineralogy and zoology, notably on birds and microscopic organisms.

Pointe Riche

Riche took an active part in the natural-history investigations carried out in Van Diemen’s Land. During the survey of the D’Entrecasteaux Channel in May 1792, he worked on several occasions alongside Labillardière. On 20 May, the two naturalists notably explored Partridge Island, off the southern part of Bruny Island. Their interests were not identical, however: Labillardière devoted himself primarily to botany, while Riche focused more closely on zoology, without neglecting mineralogical observations and field collecting. These excursions show that Pointe Riche lies within an area where the naturalist himself participated in the expedition’s scientific work.

Labillardière remembered his colleague as a naturalist deeply committed to his work. The plant Richea glauca, which Labillardière named in Riche’s honour, was later assigned to another genus, but the name Richea was adopted again in 1810 by Robert Brown for a genus of Australian plants that still bears the name today.

Cape Riche

In the pages following his account of the December 1792 incident, when Riche became lost on the Land of Nuyts, d’Entrecasteaux reflected at length on the organisational difficulties it revealed. He did not question the importance of natural-history research, but stressed that its requirements could not always be reconciled with those of the boats engaged in hydrographic surveying. The boats had to survey the coastline, sometimes some distance offshore, and could not remain indefinitely at one place waiting for those who had been put ashore.

The commander explained that he therefore sought to land the naturalists, together with the people designated to accompany them, on the most accessible parts of the coast and to arrange a precise rendezvous for their return. He emphasised the difficulties that could arise from requests that did not take sufficient account of navigational constraints and safety. In his view, the Riche incident showed precisely what could happen when the time required for scientific observation exceeded that allowed by maritime operations.

His judgement was severe, but it also reflected the responsibility he bore as commander. D’Entrecasteaux pointed out that the officers responsible for hydrographic work had to take account of the winds, sea conditions, the distances travelled and the need to return to the ships. He feared that a prolonged stay ashore might endanger the naturalists themselves or force the boats to interrupt their work.

Riche’s own report, however, shows why these limits could be difficult to observe. In terrain entirely new to him, every valley, rock formation, plant or trace of animal life could justify another stop. His account portrays the naturalist’s work as a slow and discontinuous progression, repeatedly diverted by new observations. Scientific collecting could therefore lead almost naturally to a loss of awareness of both time and distance.

The episode of December 1792 thus reveals two different but equally essential sets of priorities within the expedition. Riche’s were those of exploration and collecting, based on the need to spend time examining unfamiliar terrain. D’Entrecasteaux’s were those of command: ensuring the safety of his men, coordinating the boats and continuing the surveys without placing the ships at risk. Between these two perspectives, the crew’s mobilisation in the search for Riche shows that such tensions did not exclude personal attachment or a strong sense of solidarity among the members of the expedition.

The name Cape Riche thus preserved the memory not only of a naturalist whose scientific zeal could lead him to take considerable risks, but also of an episode in which scientific research, maritime discipline and the solidarity of the crew were closely intertwined.
