Baie Denon - Fowlers Bay

The coast extending from Point Sinclair to Fowlers Bay and Point Fowler, facing the Great Australian Bight, is marked by a succession of headlands resting on ancient rocks resistant to erosion. These separate long sandy beaches, in places backed by cliffs of consolidated sandy deposits rising to about fifty metres, together with shore platforms and extensive dune systems. Much of the shoreline formed in softer sedimentary deposits remains highly dynamic and has continued to evolve since the Baudin expedition first observed and charted it.

In low-lying coastal depressions behind the shoreline, the accumulation and evaporation of saline water also promoted the precipitation of salts and the formation of substantial gypsum deposits.

To name the geographical features surveyed by Le Géographe along this section of coast, Péron and the authors of the nomenclature selected the names of several celebrated artists. The sequence — Rubens, Vernet, Vien, Gérard, Poussin, Le Brun, Van Spaendonck and David — thus inscribed the Australian landscape within French and European artistic culture at a time when the Consulate and Empire were making art, museums and monumental history painting major instruments of prestige and political representation.

Péron wrote:

“The Îles Rubens lie a short distance from Cap Vien; they are very small, or rather, they are little more than a heap of large rocks surrounded by dangerous breakers.”

The description was intended above all to warn navigators: Péron emphasised both the small size of the Îles Rubens and the danger posed by the breakers surrounding them.

He then noted a gradual transformation in the appearance of the coast:

“However, as one continues westward, the character of the land can gradually be seen to change; already, abreast of Cap Vien, some traces of a second line of inland hills become visible, while the generally less sombre colour of the land assumes here and there slightly greenish tones. The configuration of the shore becomes still more complex near Cap Gérard […] From this point to Baie Denon, the continent presents a succession of more or less projecting capes, which have been named Cap Le Poussin, Cap Lebrun and Cap Van-Spaendonck […]”

Péron’s observation corresponds reasonably well with the present morphology of the area. Westward, the coastline becomes increasingly varied, alternating dunes, calcarenite cliffs, reefs, small beaches and more or less prominent headlands. His “succession of more or less projecting capes” can still be recognised in the numerous small coastal projections that punctuate the shore towards Fowlers Bay. His perception of inland hills and greener tones was necessarily made from offshore, but it also reflects a genuine change in the appearance of the landscape.

Writing about Baie Denon, Péron added:

“Baie Denon measures four to five miles across, with an almost equal depth; various openings seem to indicate, towards the head of the bay, the existence of some inland stream, or more probably some inlet of salt water extending more or less deeply inland and ending […] in extensive marshes.”

This interpretation was remarkably perceptive. No substantial permanent river enters Fowlers Bay, but the land immediately behind it does contain saline depressions and temporary wetlands behind the dunes. Péron’s hypothesis of a salt-water inlet extending inland and associated with marshes therefore corresponds quite closely with the morphology of the area.

Rubens and the Representation of Power
The Coronation of Marie de’ Medici at Saint-Denis, 13 May 1610, after Peter Paul Rubens. Rubens gave representations of monarchical power a theatrical scale based on movement, colour, allegory and monumentality. During the Revolutionary wars, several major works from the Southern Netherlands were transferred to Paris. Their presence in the national collections made Rubens a particularly visible reference for French artists.

David and the Representation of the New Dynasty
The Coronation of Emperor Napoleon I and Empress Joséphine at Notre-Dame, 2 December 1804, print after Jacques-Louis David. Between 1805 and 1807 David produced a monumental composition intended to establish an official image of the new regime. Napoleon is shown crowning Joséphine under the gaze of Pope Pius VII, while dignitaries, members of the imperial family, diplomats, artists and intellectuals form a vast gallery of the new imperial order. Laetitia Bonaparte, Napoleon’s mother, did not attend the ceremony and was in Rome at the time. Her absence reflected tensions with her son, but also her longstanding dislike of Joséphine, whom she had never fully accepted, notably because she was older than Napoleon. David nevertheless gave her a prominent place in the gallery, thereby transforming the scene into an idealised representation of the unity and continuity of the new imperial dynasty.

Carle Vernet and Military Heroisation
Napoleon Giving Orders before the Battle of Austerlitz, 2 December 1805, print after Carle Vernet, engraved by Samuel Cholet, c. 1808. Napoleon is shown on his horse Cyrus (renamed Austerlitz after the battle) surrounded by his officers in a moment of controlled calm before the battle. The composition thus turns military command into an almost ceremonial scene: the Emperor appears not amid the violence of combat, but as the composed and confident commander preparing victory. Carle Vernet played an important part in this visualisation of the Napoleonic campaigns. His military scenes, in which horses often occupy a central role, helped disseminate a heroic and immediately recognisable image of Napoleon and his generals.

Gérard and the Imperial Family Gallery
Caroline, Queen of Naples, and her Children, François Gérard, 1809–1810. François Gérard became one of the regime’s leading portraitists. He received commissions that contributed to the creation of a virtual gallery of the imperial family: Napoleon’s sisters, sisters-in-law, wives and female relatives were represented as princesses and sovereigns of the new Imperial Europe. His portraits thus helped give the regime a dynastic image, complementing David’s monumental history paintings.

Vigée Le Brun and Caroline Bonaparte
Marie-Annonciade-Caroline Bonaparte, Queen of Naples, with her Daughter, Élisabeth Louise Vigée Le Brun, 1807. After returning to France following her long Revolutionary exile, Vigée Le Brun was called upon in 1807 to paint Caroline Bonaparte. The commission brought the former portraitist of Marie Antoinette face to face with the new imperial dynasty. In her Memoirs, however, the artist described difficult sittings and a less than cordial relationship with Caroline. Some secondary accounts also mention disagreements with Napoleon over the portrait and its cost. The work therefore illustrates both the continuity and the tensions between the aristocratic culture of the Ancien Régime and the new courtly world created by the Bonapartes.

Van Spaendonck: Art in the Service of Science
Gaillardia pulchella / Virgilia helioides, Gérard van Spaendonck, watercolour on vellum. The drawing had both an aesthetic and scientific purpose: the plant was represented with great precision so that the characteristics useful for its identification and classification could be clearly observed. Van Spaendonck therefore embodies one of the essential dimensions of the Baudin expedition: the union of scientific observation and artistic representation. This helps explain particularly well the presence of his name in the nomenclature. Péron himself called him a “learned professor”, praising both the quality of his own work and the large number of artists he had trained in the service of natural science.

Denon, Art and Empire
Two Wounded Soldiers, engraving by Dominique-Vivant Denon. One soldier uses his uninjured hand to feed a companion whose arms appear to be disabled. A notice displayed on the wall refers to the imperial decree of 15 August 1809 granting financial assistance (500 francs) to French soldiers who had lost limbs during the campaigns in Germany and Austria. Denon stood at the centre of Napoleonic artistic policy. His activities as draughtsman, engraver, traveller and, above all, director of the Musée Napoléon directly connected art, contemporary history and the representation of the regime. Baie Denon therefore provides a particularly meaningful conclusion to this sequence of place names devoted to the arts.
