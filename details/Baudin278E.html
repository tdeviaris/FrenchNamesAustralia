Jean-Denis Barbié du Bocage, Louis-Mathieu Langlès and Pascal-François-Joseph Gossellin approached the collection, interpretation and representation of geographical knowledge in different but complementary ways. Their activities stood at the intersection of scholarship, the great national collections and the changing practical needs of government administration.

Barbié du Bocage: a map for public education
In 1804, Jean-Denis Barbié du Bocage prepared, by order of the Minister of the Interior, a Map of Europe intended for public education. The work reflects the importance then attached to cartography as an educational tool, while also illustrating Barbié du Bocage’s position between scholarly geography and government service. His work in ministerial map depots gave him access to maps, travel accounts, ancient texts and information gathered through diplomatic and scholarly networks, allowing him to compare and combine a wide range of geographical sources.

Langlès: languages, itineraries and the geography of Asia
Louis-Mathieu Langlès brought to geography a detailed knowledge of Asian languages and sources. A map published in 1802, devoted to the countries between the sources of the Ganges and the Caspian Sea, associates his name with that of the naturalist and traveller Johann Reinhold Forster. It notably traces the route of Alexander the Great and brings together information derived from different geographical traditions and systems of measurement. Langlès’s command of Oriental languages gave him access to geographical information contained in sources that had previously been difficult for European scholars to use.

Gossellin: recovering the measurements of ancient geographers
Pascal-François-Joseph Gossellin devoted much of his research to ancient geography and to the systems of measurement employed by Greek and Roman authors. He sought, in particular, to understand how distances expressed in stadia or Roman miles could be compared and translated into the units used in modern geography.

His Atlas ou recueil de cartes géographiques, published in 1814, formed part of this research. A map devoted to the Fretum Herculeum, the Strait of Gibraltar of Greco-Roman geography, shows, for example, how Gossellin attempted to reconstruct information derived from ancient geography and relate it to a modern cartographic representation.
