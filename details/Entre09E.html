D'Entrecasteaux was chosen to command the expedition in search of La Pérouse largely because of his experience of long-distance navigation. A few years earlier, while commanding La Résolution in the Indian and Asian seas, he had undertaken a difficult voyage to Canton. When the expedition was organised in 1791, his royal instructions explicitly referred to his "talents" and "experience". His mission combined the search for La Pérouse with a broad programme of geographical reconnaissance and work intended to benefit "navigation, geography, commerce, the arts and sciences".

The recognition of Bruny Island and of the channel separating it from the main island of Tasmania did not, however, result from a plan established in advance. In April 1792, the two ships initially intended to make for Adventure Bay, on the eastern coast of present-day Bruny Island. D'Entrecasteaux was at the time recovering from a fall in which several ribs had been broken and had to direct the navigation from his cabin, relying on bearings reported to him by his officers.

Labillardière relates that an error by Willaumez may have played a decisive part. Asked for the bearing of the Eddystone, Willaumez is said to have reported it as 19° west of south rather than 19° east. Believing that he recognised Adventure Bay, d'Entrecasteaux therefore ordered the ships towards a bay on their left. In this way they entered the bay that became known as Recherche Bay. The expedition's accounts do not all present the sequence of events in exactly the same way, however, and d'Entrecasteaux himself placed greater emphasis on the subsequent observations made by his officers.

Reconnaissances carried out by Crétin and Beautemps-Beaupré soon showed that the waters extending north from the bay were not simply a coastal inlet. Their observations appeared to indicate "the entrance to a very deep channel". D'Entrecasteaux therefore sent boats to investigate further before continuing the planned voyage. When one of the first parties returned from this reconnaissance, he recorded that the men came back "with cries of joy". He described the anchorage as so vast and secure that, in his words, all the navies of the world could have assembled there.

The reconnaissance had an important geographical consequence: it established that the land lying east of the passage, on which Adventure Bay was situated, formed a separate island. A section of coastline that had previously been imperfectly represented on European charts could now be surveyed as a true sea channel separating Bruny Island from the rest of Van Diemen's Land. Beautemps-Beaupré was able to undertake its systematic survey, giving this part of the coast a new degree of cartographic precision.

The names themselves preserve the memory of the expedition's commander. Bruny was his family name, while under the Ancien Régime d'Entrecasteaux referred to the marquisate of Entrecasteaux in Provence, which belonged to his family. Bruny Island, Cape Bruny, D'Entrecasteaux Channel in Tasmania and Point d'Entrecasteaux in Western Australia therefore commemorate the same man under different forms of his name.

The spelling of his name nevertheless varied during the revolutionary and post-revolutionary periods. The particle d', associated with the former nobility, was frequently omitted or incorporated into the surname. The forms Dentrecasteaux and Bruny-Dentrecasteaux consequently occur in contemporary documents and in the official publication of the voyage: Rossel's account of 1808 was entitled Voyage de Dentrecasteaux, while the royal instructions reproduced in the same volume refer to the commander as Bruny-Dentrecasteaux. Beautemps-Beaupré's atlas was likewise published as the Atlas du voyage de Bruny-Dentrecasteaux.

The same variation can be found in place names: D'Entrecasteaux Channel also appears in French sources as canal Dentrecasteaux. These forms therefore do not refer to different people or places, but reflect changing conventions in the use of the name between the Ancien Régime, the revolutionary period and publications of the early nineteenth century.
