Pointe Maugé - Point Mauge

Île Maurouard - St Helens Island

René Maugé already knew Nicolas Baudin before the departure for the southern lands. Like Anselme Riedlé, the expedition's head gardener and specialist in plants, he had taken part in Baudin's previous voyage aboard La Belle Angélique and later the Fanny, between 1796 and 1798. Riedlé and Maugé thus belonged to the small group of experienced collaborators whom Baudin wished to have with him again on his new expedition. Their relationship with the commander went beyond purely professional ties and rested on trust developed over several years of shared navigation and scientific work.

Maugé played an important role in the zoological research of the voyage. As a collector and natural-history preparator, he worked closely with François Péron and Charles-Alexandre Lesueur. One episode related by Péron illustrates this collaboration particularly well. While the naturalists were attempting to obtain the unusual pelagic organisms that Péron would study under the name Pyrosoma atlanticum, “M. Maugé, among us, was the first who succeeded in obtaining them”: within a short time he collected more than thirty or forty specimens, enabling Péron to observe their form, organisation and remarkable phosphorescence. The species was formally described by Péron in 1804 under the name Pyrosoma atlanticum.

Maugé also devoted a substantial part of his work to birds. He collected and prepared numerous specimens during the voyage, particularly at Timor. These collections helped document the avifauna of the regions visited and later provided material for naturalists working on the scientific results of the expedition. Several zoological names subsequently perpetuated his memory, among them that of the Barred Dove.

This scientific activity was interrupted by illness contracted during the voyage. At Timor, a severe outbreak of dysentery affected both the crews and the scientific staff. Anselme Riedlé, Baudin and Maugé's former companion on La Belle Angélique, died at Kupang on 21 October 1801. His death deeply affected Baudin, who regarded him as one of his close collaborators and entrusted his personal effects to Maugé. Maugé himself had contracted the disease and never recovered his strength. His condition continued to deteriorate during the passage towards Van Diemen's Land.

At Maria Island in February 1802, Maugé nevertheless attempted to continue his work despite his already greatly weakened condition. Péron relates that he still took part in a fishing excursion around Bruny Island and brought back more than twenty new species of fish, together with several rare shells. Exhausted, he nevertheless attempted to go ashore the following day to search for specimens himself. He had barely reached the beach when he fainted and had to be hurriedly carried back on board in a state of extreme weakness. He never went ashore again after this episode and died on 20 February 1802. In Péron's account, this final collecting effort appears as the last expression of Maugé's commitment to the natural-history work of the expedition.

The following day, while Péron and Boullanger were completing their operations in Oyster Bay, they heard cannon shots from the ships and realised that they announced the funeral of their companion. Péron records that Maugé was “universally regretted on both vessels.” His body was buried on Maria Island at the foot of a large eucalyptus tree, against which a lead plaque was fixed recording the circumstances of his death. Péron states that “the name Pointe Maugé was given to this part of the island, where the remains of our unfortunate companion were laid to rest.”

The place name therefore has an unusually direct connection with the man it commemorates: Pointe Maugé marks the site of his burial. Within only a few months, the death of Riedlé at Timor followed by that of Maugé at Maria Island deprived Baudin of two long-standing collaborators to whom he was deeply attached.
