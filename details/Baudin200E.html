In 1800, Liancourt helped establish, through private subscriptions, a society and a Central Vaccination Committee responsible for assessing and promoting the new preventive method. In 1804, the government created the Society for the Extinction of Smallpox in France through the Propagation of Vaccination. Its administrators included physicians, scientists and public figures such as Berthollet, Laplace, Fontanes, Lacépède, Corvisart and Hallé. Vaccination was made available free of charge to children from poor families. On 11 May 1811, Napoleon’s son, the King of Rome, was vaccinated at the Château de Saint-Cloud by Dr Henri-Marie Husson.

The school founded by Liancourt was transferred to Compiègne in 1800 and then to Châlons-sur-Marne in 1806. It developed into a school of arts and trades intended to train technicians and supervisors not only for the army but also for France’s expanding industries. Its curriculum combined theoretical subjects, particularly mathematics and technical drawing, with practical workshop training. Liancourt served as its inspector general, while scientists including Berthollet, Laplace and Monge participated in its administration. The present-day Arts et Métiers engineering institution traces its origins to Liancourt’s school.
