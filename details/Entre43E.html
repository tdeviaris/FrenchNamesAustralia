Île Willaumez - Betsey Island

First extract from Willaumez’s journal - 14 February 1793

On 14 February 1793, Willaumez recorded:

“I was sent by the General, who ordered me to determine whether there was a passage to the north, north-east, between the Maria Islands [and] the mainland; accordingly, at 1:40, we left the ship with M. Beaupré, geographical engineer, and nine men in the large boat, provisioned with four days of complete rations in drink [and] vegetables, two days of water and eight days of biscuit…”

Second extract from Willaumez’s journal - 19 February 1793

“On the 19th, wind W.S.W., a fresh breeze, overcast weather and rain until 2 o’clock. The sea was heavy for a boat, and we tacked. Having nevertheless gained a little ground, we shortened sail near the island [N] and rowed under its shelter as far as its western point, from where we took the 120 bearing. After tacking for some time, we shipped the oars to keep ourselves moving during the lulls. At 5 o’clock, being one mile to leeward of the rocky point, we shortened sail; by the strength of the oars, we succeeded in passing between two of these rocks and reaching more sheltered water. Once to the westward, we set sail again and stood for the strait, which we entered at half past seven, after five days’ absence…”

Willaumez already had considerable navigational experience when he joined the expedition. He had previously served under d’Entrecasteaux aboard the Patriote and joined him again, with the rank of enseigne de vaisseau, on the expedition in search of La Pérouse. During the voyage, he was promoted to lieutenant de vaisseau and was entrusted, among other duties, with monitoring the marine timekeepers, which were essential for determining longitude.

His expertise extended well beyond piloting. Willaumez was among the officers experienced in the astronomical observations required for navigation. The expedition used instruments including sextants and Borda’s reflecting circle. He was particularly familiar with this type of instrument. Even before the d’Entrecasteaux voyage, his skill in nautical astronomy had earned him a reflecting circle presented by Louis XVI and bearing the inscription “Presented by the King to M. Willaumez, First Pilot.” During the expedition, his experience as both navigator and observer thus closely complemented the hydrographic work of Beautemps-Beaupré, as demonstrated in particular by their boat reconnaissance of February 1793.
