About the image
Lesueur depicts these island emus as a family group. Two young birds, described in the original caption as twenty to twenty-five days old and still bearing striped plumage, gather beside a reclining adult, while another adult appears to stand guard. A third juvenile, about five weeks old and beginning to lose its stripes, can be seen in the background.

This family arrangement does not entirely correspond to what is now known of emu behaviour. In emus, the male incubates the eggs and subsequently protects and raises the chicks. Lesueur’s image is therefore both a valuable natural-history record and a partly interpretative reconstruction shaped by the zoological knowledge of the period.

The “cassowaries” observed by the French were in fact emus native to Kangaroo Island, now known as Kangaroo Island emus. Morphological and genetic research places them very close to the mainland emu, of which they formed a slightly smaller island population. Still numerous when the Baudin expedition visited, they probably disappeared during the first decades of the nineteenth century as a result of hunting and changes to their habitat. Genetic studies confirm the close evolutionary relationship between the island populations and the mainland emu, while indicating that their reduced body size resulted from insular dwarfism.
