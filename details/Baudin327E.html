Étienne Lenoir and the establishment of the metric system

Alongside his contribution to improvements in navigational instruments used during major maritime expeditions, Étienne Lenoir played an essential role in the establishment of the metric system. Regarded as the leading French scientific instrument maker of his time, he ran a workshop employing seven craftsmen. He manufactured the repeating circles designed by Charles de Borda and used by Jean-Baptiste Delambre and Pierre Méchain between 1792 and 1798 to measure the meridian arc extending from Dunkirk to Barcelona. This major geodetic survey contributed to determining the length of the metre, defined in relation to the dimensions of the Earth.

One of the four repeating circles made by Lenoir around 1791–1792, bearing the number IV, is preserved at the Marseille Observatory. Made of brass, the instrument stands 77 cm high and has a graduated circle 36 cm in diameter. It bears the engraved inscription “Lenoir à Paris”. Its two telescopes were probably made by the optician Noël-Jean Lerebours. By allowing the same angle to be measured repeatedly without resetting the instrument to zero, the repeating circle reduced the effect of certain measurement errors and improved the accuracy of geodetic surveys.

Lenoir also manufactured standard metre measures and instruments for comparing units of measurement under the new metric system. In 1814, he was appointed an instrument-maker member of the Bureau des longitudes, in recognition of his craftsmanship and contribution to scientific progress. His son, Paul-Marie-Étienne Lenoir, succeeded him as head of the workshop.

Lenoir's work thus illustrates the crucial role played by instrument makers in scientific advances during the late eighteenth century, when the accuracy of astronomical observations, geographical surveys and terrestrial measurements depended closely on the quality of the instruments employed.
