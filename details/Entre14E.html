Île Lahaye - Hope Island

The name Île La Haye commemorates the important role played by gardeners in the great scientific expeditions, whose responsibilities extended far beyond caring for the plants carried on board. Félix Lahaye (1767–1829), also known as Delahaye, began working at an early age in the botanical garden of the Rouen Academy of Sciences. In 1788, he entered the botanical school of the Jardin du Roi in Paris, where he trained under the botanist André Thouin. Three years later, he was appointed gardener to d'Entrecasteaux's expedition and placed under the supervision of Labillardière for his botanical work.

Gardeners accompanying scientific voyages possessed considerable botanical expertise. Lahaye accompanied Labillardière on collecting excursions, but also assembled his own collections and kept detailed horticultural notes. During the expedition's various stopovers, his responsibilities included not only collecting seeds and plants but also ensuring their survival during long sea voyages.

He devised a system of notation to record the geographical origin of seeds, the nature of the soil in which the plants grew and their botanical characteristics. In a notebook intended to guide their subsequent cultivation, he combined letters of the alphabet with numbers to distinguish, among other things, annuals from perennials, trees from shrubs, and plants growing on plains from those found in sandy soils (ANF/MAR/5JJ/2/24).

He also arranged the collected seeds in fifteen specially prepared boxes or cases, those from New Holland being grouped together in box no. 3 and box no. 8 (ANF/MAR/5JJ/2/30).

In Van Diemen's Land, Lahaye carefully recorded his observations on the climate and vegetation. In the notes accompanying the seeds collected between April and May 1792, he described a mountainous country with a few damp plains and emphasised the cool temperatures. He noted in particular that the thermometer had fallen to seven degrees above freezing inside his tent (ANF/MAR/5JJ/2/26).

During this first visit, he also sowed some of the vegetable seeds brought from France in the small garden he attempted to establish at Port du Nord (see the entry for Port du Nord, under “Further Information”). In his journal, he provided a detailed account of his initial efforts:

“On my arrival I sowed some cress which germinated well after three days; after eight days two men and myself tilled, with great difficulty, a piece of land measuring 28 feet square. I sowed plants suitable for the season, which are celery, chervil, chicory, cabbages, grey romaine lettuce, different kinds of turnips, white onion, radishes, sorrel, peas, black salsify and potatoes.

I had large quantities sown everywhere in the woods, in the more open spaces and where the soil was more friable. It was not possible to sow any more in the soil which is very difficult to cultivate, and in the season which did not allow it. I sowed mixed seeds everywhere, thrown at random, where I believed they could succeed.”

Later, in Tonga, in 1793, the expedition collected 200 young breadfruit plants intended for transportation to French settlements in the Indian Ocean. The cultivated varieties prized in Polynesia were seedless and could therefore only be propagated vegetatively, particularly through root suckers or cuttings. Lahaye placed the plants in specially constructed boxes fitted with drainage holes and protective frames that allowed them to be sheltered or ventilated according to the weather conditions. Despite the difficulties of the voyage, several plants survived as far as Java and were eventually transported to the Isle de France, now Mauritius.

His botanical collecting was equally substantial. His herbarium comprised 2,699 dried and numbered plant specimens gathered during the expedition and his prolonged stay in the Indian Ocean. Following the upheavals caused by d'Entrecasteaux's death and the detention of part of the expedition by the Dutch in Java, Lahaye continued to care for the breadfruit plants. He did not return to France until 1797.

His career subsequently continued in some of France's most prestigious gardens. In 1798, he became head gardener at Trianon, and in 1805 took up the same position at Malmaison, the estate of Empress Joséphine. He died in 1829. His herbarium, catalogue and journal were acquired by the Muséum national d'histoire naturelle in 1879.
