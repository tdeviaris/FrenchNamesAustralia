Le Coin-de-Mire - an islet in the Curtis Group

Le Cône - Cone Islet, Curtis Group

Le Coin-de-Mire and Le Cône are two descriptive place names given by Nicolas Baudin to rocky islets in Bass Strait during the expedition's second passage towards the southern coast of New Holland in late 1802. They lie in a particularly intricate sector, scattered with islands, rocks and reefs, west of the Kent Group. Surveys carried out during the expedition were subsequently incorporated into the cartographic work prepared for the Voyage de découvertes aux Terres Australes. The surviving manuscript material preserves several successive stages in the development of both the charts and their nomenclature. The chart held by the Archives nationales as MAP/6JJ/5/B, item 112a, notably shows the tracks of the Naturaliste and the Casuarina through Bass Strait.

On 2 December 1802, after sighting the Casuarina and the Fanny, an American vessel, far to the east at daybreak, Baudin decided not to wait for them. The Géographe, accompanied by the Naturaliste, then attempted to work its way out of the archipelago into which the ships had become engaged, which Baudin placed about six leagues west of the Kent Group. He described their passage between two islets:

“We passed through the channel formed by the islet which I named Le Coin de Mire and the one bearing the name Les Deux Frères.”

The name Coin-de-Mire appears to refer directly to the distinctive shape of the islet. In artillery terminology, a coin de mire was a wedge-shaped device used to adjust the elevation and aim of a cannon. The clearly defined outline of the islet, standing out among the numerous rocks and islands in this crowded part of the strait, therefore probably accounts for Baudin's choice of this descriptive name. Its visibility would also have made it a useful sea mark for navigators.

After passing Le Coin-de-Mire and Les Deux Frères, Baudin continued towards the south-west. Another islet, particularly striking in its appearance, then attracted his attention. He named it Le Cône, directly after its shape:

“[...] but without any hope of being able to round the islet of Le Cône, a large and very remarkable rock, whose view and position I give on my chart from the Promontory to Bass Port.”

Baudin thus specified that both the profile of the rock and its position had been recorded on his chart. Like La Brioche and Le Coin-de-Mire, Le Cône is therefore a descriptive toponym derived from the appearance of a conspicuous sea mark as observed from offshore. The name was retained in the cartography produced from the expedition.

As the ships approached Le Cône, they also encountered a hydrographic danger that apparently had not yet been shown on existing charts. Baudin continued:

“As we drew nearer to this islet, we became aware of a reef just awash, which is all the more dangerous when tacking in these waters because it is of only limited extent [...].”

The heavy breaking of the sea nevertheless allowed the reef to be detected in sufficient time. Baudin immediately ordered the necessary bearings to be taken “for the safety of navigators”. The episode shows that the December 1802 passage was not merely a transit through Bass Strait: it also provided an opportunity to supplement and refine the hydrographic observations made during the expedition's earlier passages.

At noon, Baudin recorded the position of the Géographe as 39° 24' 45" S and 144° 25' 56" E of the Paris meridian. After several changes of tack, the vessel succeeded in rounding Le Coin-de-Mire and then passed close to the reef, which Baudin estimated had been approached to within no more than three-quarters of a league.
