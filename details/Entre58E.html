Cap Le Grand - Cape Le Grand

Pointe Le Grand - Kinghorne Point

Jacques-Bertrand Le Grand (1763-1798), an officer aboard L’Espérance, played an active role in the expedition’s navigation and coastal reconnaissance. His journals reveal an officer attentive not only to the ships’ course, but also to winds, currents, depths, the nature of the seabed, and the possibilities for anchorage. He was promoted to lieutenant de vaisseau in 1793 and then to captain de vaisseau in 1794.

In Van Diemen’s Land in early 1793, his journal offers a detailed view of this everyday practice of navigation in the D’Entrecasteaux Channel. As the expedition proceeded northwards after leaving Recherche Bay, Le Grand carefully recorded soundings, shoals, changes in the wind, and the manoeuvres required of the two frigates. On 13 February, La Recherche and L’Espérance touched a bank of fine sand and mud that had not been identified during the earlier surveys. Le Grand noted that the sea was so calm that the grounding was almost imperceptible. When the water level rose, the ships were able to free themselves and continue on their way. The following day, when the breeze again failed, he recorded an anchorage in thirty-two feet of water over a muddy bottom, as well as the departure of a boat sent to reconnoitre the neighbouring bays. His journal thus reveals a constant reading of the route, in which every sounding, variation in the wind, or change in the seabed could influence the handling of the ships.

A few months earlier, in December 1792, this skill had been tested under far more dangerous circumstances on the southern coast of New Holland. L’Espérance, caught in severe weather and surrounded by hazards, was desperately seeking an anchorage. From high in the rigging, Le Grand saw that an island to port formed a recess with the mainland that might provide shelter. He wrote that, in this moment of “cruel perplexity,” he had identified an anchorage that appeared sufficiently safe to attempt, despite the dangers that had to be passed.

A marginal note in his journal makes clear the responsibility entrusted to him at that moment: Le Grand was placed in charge of the course and manoeuvring required to bring L’Espérance into the anchorage he had selected from the rigging. He later considered himself fortunate to have “chosen well the only place that could save our lives.”

The following day, once the weather had improved, he was sent to sound the bay formed by the island beside which the men had found refuge. Rather than unnecessarily expose the boat or those entrusted to his care, he swam ashore himself. There he observed numerous seals and sea lions, as well as groups of grey geese with greenish beaks, now identified as Cape Barren Geese.

Le Grand’s journals thus reveal two complementary dimensions of the work of a naval officer engaged in hydrographic surveying and navigation: the methodical vigilance required for the daily reconnaissance of still poorly known coasts and, when circumstances demanded it, the ability to interpret the coastline, seabed, and state of the sea quickly enough to choose a course or an anchorage. The place names Pointe Le Grand in Tasmania and Cape Le Grand in Western Australia preserve the memory of his contribution to the expedition’s hydrographic work and navigational decisions.
