Eolia Dubayle’s study of eighteenth-century naval surgery helps to show the scale of Joannet’s work. For the Espérance, she identified 481 consultations between October 1791 and April 1794, averaging more than fifteen consultations per month, although the number varied greatly according to the different stages of the voyage.

Digestive disorders occupy a particularly important place in his notebooks: diarrhoea, colic, indigestion, nausea, intestinal pain and, later, dysentery. Treatments were still strongly influenced by humoral medicine and frequently involved purgatives, enemas, tamarind, ipecacuanha or jalap, as well as lemonade with cream of tartar, which also appears in Joannet’s prescriptions in Tasmania.

As the voyage continued, however, the length of the passages and the deterioration of food and water increasingly exceeded the resources available to the surgeon. Digestive illnesses and scurvy became more frequent, and during the voyage towards the Moluccas in 1793 Joannet sometimes ran short of remedies. When fresh provisions again became available, patients were given, among other things, lemon juice, vegetables and turtle broth.

Joannet’s relations with the expedition’s naturalists also appear at times to have been strained. Some of them were irritated by his claims to be regarded as a naturalist and by his asserted knowledge of botany. Yet botanical knowledge was closely connected with his medical work, since much of the pharmacopoeia he used still depended on plants, decoctions, infusions, juices and extracts.

His medical notebooks therefore provide a valuable counterpoint to the expedition’s sea journals: they show not only where the ships travelled, but also the physical condition of the men who carried out the voyage.
