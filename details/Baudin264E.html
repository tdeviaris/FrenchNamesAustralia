Baie Murat - Murat Bay
Cap Murat - Point Murat

Few figures experienced such a spectacular rise in society as Joachim Murat (1767-1815). Born in La Bastide-Fortunière, in the Quercy region of France (now Labastide-Murat), the youngest of eleven children born to a couple of innkeepers, he was originally destined for an ecclesiastical career. After studying at the college in Cahors, he entered the Lazarist seminary in Toulouse, from which he was expelled in 1787. He then enlisted in the cavalry. The French Revolution was to offer him career opportunities scarcely imaginable under the Ancien Régime.

His encounter with Bonaparte during the royalist uprising of 13 Vendémiaire, Year IV (5 October 1795), marked a turning point in his career. He subsequently distinguished himself during the Italian and Egyptian campaigns, notably at Aboukir in 1799, and played an active role in the coup of 18 Brumaire. In January 1800, a few months before the departure of the Baudin expedition, he married Caroline Bonaparte, the youngest sister of the First Consul.

Murat stood out among Napoleon's generals for his extraordinary panache. An accomplished horseman of remarkable physical courage, he cultivated an instantly recognisable appearance: long curly hair, elaborate headgear adorned with tall plumes, lavishly embroidered uniforms, furs and vivid colours. His taste for extravagant attire, sometimes ridiculed by his contemporaries, helped shape his legend. His official portraits, particularly those painted by Antoine-Jean Gros and François Gérard, reflect this carefully cultivated public image.

At the time François Péron was preparing the expedition's narrative for publication, Murat was at the height of his military glory. A Marshal of the Empire from 1804, Prince and Grand Admiral of the Empire in 1805, and Grand Duke of Berg and Cleves in 1806, he had distinguished himself at Austerlitz and during the Prussian campaign. In February 1807, his famous cavalry charge at Eylau helped turn the tide at a particularly critical moment for the Napoleonic army.

It was against this backdrop of military victories, the rise of new elites emerging from the Revolution and the consolidation of the imperial dynasty that Murat's name was bestowed upon two widely separated geographical features along the Australian coastline. The names Baie Murat and Cap Murat, recorded in the first volume of Péron's account published in 1807, reflect Murat's prominent position in Napoleonic France, although the precise reasons for this double tribute remain unknown.

Having become King of Naples in 1808, Murat did not confine himself to his role as a military leader. He continued the administrative and legal modernisation of the kingdom and gradually sought to assert his independence from Napoleon. In March 1815, in his Rimini Proclamation, he called upon the Italians to unite and achieve their independence. This initiative, which also stemmed from his need to preserve his throne, subsequently earned him recognition as one of the forerunners of the Risorgimento.

His life came to a tragic end on 13 October 1815: following an attempt to regain his kingdom, he was executed by firing squad at Pizzo, in Calabria.

From an innkeeper's son to a king, Murat's remarkable trajectory illustrates the opportunities for social advancement opened up by the French Revolution, the emergence of a new aristocracy under the Empire, and the fragility of the thrones created by Napoleon.
