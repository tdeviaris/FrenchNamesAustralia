Cap Kersaint - Cape Kersaint

Armand-Guy-Simon’s father, Guy-François de Coëtnempren de Kersaint (1703–1759), served in the French Royal Navy during the wars of the mid-eighteenth century. He perished with almost the entire crew of the Thésée at the Battle of Quiberon Bay on 20 November 1759. The vessel foundered while manoeuvring in heavy seas, after water entered through the gunports of her lower battery.

Armand-Guy-Simon de Coëtnempren, Comte de Kersaint (1742–1793), entered the Navy at an early age and served during the Seven Years’ War and the naval campaigns of the American War of Independence. He operated in the Caribbean alongside Guichen—see Baie de Guichen—and in 1782 commanded a division sent against several colonial settlements in Guiana then occupied by the British. The operation resulted in the capitulation of Demerara, Essequibo and Berbice. This campaign is sometimes described imprecisely as a conquest of Surinam, although these territories formed separate colonies.

Kersaint also devoted a considerable part of his career to the technical and administrative problems of the Navy. He submitted numerous proposals concerning ship construction and equipment, copper sheathing, sail plans, shipboard galleys and maritime signalling. On the eastern coast of Demerara, he notably initiated the construction of an 80-foot-high timber tower intended for the transmission of signals and capable of serving as a conspicuous landmark for navigators—see the first illustration.

The sheet-metal “ventilated galleys” fitted aboard the Géographe and the Naturaliste were likewise derived from a model devised by Kersaint. The system was subsequently modified by Missiessy, who wrote:

“The iron galley, constructed on the model of Kersaint’s, with the alterations indicated by experience, is the most advantageous of all known galleys, since it is, as it were, isolated from the vessel, its only points of contact being the feet that support it, and consequently transmitting no heat.”

— See the second illustration.

Garos subsequently adapted this principle for the vessels of the Baudin expedition. These “ventilated galleys” were intended to use the effect of the fire to promote air circulation and carry stale air and smoke away from the lower parts of the ship. The alterations made to the system, however, proved defective from the beginning of the voyage.

Initially supportive of the Revolution, Kersaint was elected to the Legislative Assembly and subsequently to the National Convention. He resigned from the latter after Louis XVI was sentenced to death. Associated with the Girondins and regarded with suspicion during the Terror, he was arrested, convicted by the Revolutionary Tribunal and guillotined in Paris on 4 December 1793. By assigning his name to a headland on Kangaroo Island, the expedition’s cartographers inscribed into Australian geography the memory of an Enlightenment naval officer whose career combined command at sea, technical inquiry and political engagement.
