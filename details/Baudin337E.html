Cap Adanson - Cape Adamson

Michel Adanson was trained by some of the leading French naturalists of his time, notably René-Antoine Ferchault de Réaumur and Bernard de Jussieu. Jussieu helped him obtain a modest position with the French East India Company, enabling him to remain in Senegal from 1749 to 1754. Adanson soon conceived his stay as a scientific investigation extending far beyond botanical collecting. He studied plants and animals, collected shells and minerals, drew maps, and undertook systematic astronomical and meteorological observations. His correspondence with the Jussieu brothers reveals the remarkable range of his collections and scientific projects.

His approach placed particular emphasis on direct observation and knowledge acquired from local people. He began learning Wolof in order to gain a better understanding of the properties and uses of plants. In a letter sent from Senegal in August 1749, he explained that learning the language would help him obtain information about certain unusual plants. His manuscripts and publications reveal a broader interest in languages, social practices, economic life and local knowledge. His voyage consequently provides an important record of encounters between European natural history and African knowledge in the mid-eighteenth century.

The most famous of his botanical observations concerned the African baobab. Adanson produced one of the first detailed scientific descriptions of the complete tree based on his observations in Senegal. The genus Adansonia was subsequently named in his honour, and the African baobab received the scientific name Adansonia digitata. Adanson devoted a memoir to the tree, Description d'un Arbre d'un nouveau genre, appelé Baobab, observé au Sénégal, published in the Mémoires de l'Académie royale des sciences for the year 1761, printed in Paris in 1763.

His work in Senegal, however, extended much further. He studied indigo, gum arabic and palms, while assembling substantial zoological and botanical collections. His correspondence shows him sending plants and seeds to the Jussieus, birds and other specimens to Réaumur, while continuing experiments on indigo and astronomical observations.

Molluscs occupied a particularly important place in his research. His Histoire naturelle du Sénégal of 1757 included an extensive study of the shells he had observed and collected. Adanson sought to move beyond classifications based solely on the external appearance of the shell by paying greater attention to the organisation of the animal itself. This approach anticipated his broader project for a natural system of classification, developed in Familles des plantes (1763), in which he rejected systems based on a small number of privileged characters, such as Linnaeus's sexual system, in favour of comparisons drawing on a much wider range of characteristics.

Adanson and the Baudin expedition

The connection between Adanson and the voyage to the southern lands was more than an intellectual inheritance. In 1800, by then over seventy years old, Adanson was a member of the Institut national commission responsible for coordinating the scientific programme of Baudin's expedition. He therefore took a direct part in the discussions preceding its departure.

A particularly tangible record of this involvement is preserved at the National Library of Australia. A copy of François Péron's Observations sur l'anthropologie, submitted in 1800 in support of his application to join the voyage, bears numerous handwritten annotations by Adanson. They show that he read Péron's proposal closely, and at times very critically, particularly his reflections on “natural man” and on peoples then described by Europeans as “savages”.

Adanson thus embodied the field naturalist, traveller and theorist of classification, three dimensions central to the ambitious scientific programme entrusted to the Baudin expedition. His Senegalese experience had already demonstrated how a voyage could combine botany, zoology, meteorology, geography, the study of languages and the observation of human societies. Half a century later, Péron and the other scientists of the southern voyage pursued, in a different context and with new methods, a comparable ambition to study environments, living organisms and human societies together.
