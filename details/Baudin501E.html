André Thouin and the fate of the Baudin expedition's botanical collections

The son of the head gardener of the Jardin du Roi (Royal Garden), André Thouin (1747-1824) grew up among the botanical collections of this Parisian institution. In 1764, at the age of seventeen, he succeeded his father with the support of Buffon. When the Jardin du Roi was transformed into the Muséum national d'histoire naturelle in 1793, he became professor responsible for the cultivation and acclimatisation of foreign plants.

Thouin played an important role in the scientific preparation of the major French expeditions of the late eighteenth century. He helped prepare the gardeners who embarked on the voyages of La Pérouse, d'Entrecasteaux and Baudin. Their mission was not merely to collect botanical specimens for herbaria: they were also expected to bring back seeds and living plants that could be cultivated and propagated in Europe. Preserving these collections during voyages lasting several months required considerable horticultural expertise and suitably designed transport containers.

Thouin's plant cases: transporting living plants by sea

Long before the Baudin expedition, André Thouin had sought to overcome the difficulties involved in transporting living plants on long sea voyages. In his Mémoire pour diriger le jardinier dans les travaux de son voyage autour du monde (Instructions to guide the gardener in his work during a voyage around the world), written for the La Pérouse expedition and published in 1797, he outlined methods for collecting, preserving and transporting seeds, bulbs and living plants.

Among the devices used were enclosed plant cases, also known as “Thouin cases”. Their design, inspired by traditional sea chests, made them easier for the crews to handle. Fitted with glazed panels, wire mesh and protective shutters, they allowed ventilation and exposure to light to be regulated while protecting the plants from adverse weather and animals aboard the ship. A layer of moss could help retain moisture in the soil and keep the plants in place despite the movement of the vessel.

Thouin emphasised the need to keep trees in active growth throughout the voyage. The plants nevertheless required regular attention, watering with fresh water and care adapted to the changing climatic conditions encountered during the voyage.

Thouin and the expedition's gardeners

Thouin's role extended beyond the practical and scientific preparation of expeditions. He remained a key point of contact for the gardeners at sea, who reported to him on their collections, the difficulties they encountered and the conditions under which they attempted to implement his recommendations.

The letter addressed to him by Anselme Riedlé, head gardener aboard the Géographe, from Timor on 28 September 1801 provides valuable evidence in this respect. It reveals the gap between the objectives established by the scientists of the Muséum and the realities of a voyage of discovery. Despite more than three months of navigation along the coasts of New Holland, Riedlé had been able to go ashore on only four occasions. Nevertheless, he had succeeded in collecting 270 plant species for the herbarium.

Thouin's concerns about the transport of living plants were also reflected in this correspondence. Riedlé informed him of the difficulties arising from the limited space aboard the ship and the navigational and safety requirements stressed by Baudin. The implementation of Thouin's recommended methods thus depended largely on the practical conditions of the voyage and the opportunities afforded to the gardeners by the expedition's command.

The letter also reveals the scientific dedication of the gardener to whom Thouin had entrusted this mission. Despite being ill in Timor, Riedlé continued his botanical excursions:

“I would have recovered by now if I did not go out every day to roam the countryside; but I find it impossible to remain indoors when surrounded outside by precious plants.”

Beyond their scientific exchanges, the closing lines of the letter testify to the personal trust Riedlé placed in Thouin:

“I once again commend my wife and child to your care, and embrace them with all my heart.”

Riedlé died in Timor only a few weeks later, on 21 October 1801. In France, however, Thouin was to continue the work undertaken by the gardener, endeavouring to preserve and realise the scientific potential of the collections brought back by the expedition.

The return of the collections to France

When the Naturaliste, commanded by Hamelin, returned to Le Havre on 7 June 1803, Thouin was entrusted with receiving the botanical collections. In a letter addressed to his colleagues at the Muséum on 22 June, he made no attempt to conceal his dismay at the condition of the living plants: “Everything appeared to me to be dead, or nearly so.” Of the approximately eight hundred plants collected in Timor and New Holland, only twelve to fifteen were still growing in good condition. Thouin attributed these losses to a shortage of water, the presence of rats aboard the ship and inadequate care.

Thouin nevertheless decided to have all sixty-nine planting tubs (bailles) used to transport the plants taken to Paris. He hoped that “the good and unfortunate Riedlé”, following the instructions he had received before his departure, had placed seeds, bulbs and tubers in the soil that might subsequently grow. Several plants new to the Muséum's collections were indeed obtained in this way, including species of Opercularia, Mimosa and Casuarina.

This episode illustrates the difficulties of transporting living plants by sea, Thouin's horticultural expertise and the trust he placed in Riedlé's work.

From the Australian coastline to European botanical gardens

Thouin's work extended well beyond receiving and cataloguing the collections. Through his extensive network of correspondents, he organised the distribution of seeds to botanists, garden owners and knowledgeable amateurs, thereby increasing the opportunities for acclimatising newly introduced species.

The example of Baron Dumont de Courset (1746-1824), who had established a substantial botanical collection on his estate in the Pas-de-Calais region of northern France, illustrates how this network operated. Between 1803 and 1804, Thouin sent him seeds representing 120 Australian species. Despite the difficulties posed by the local climate, Dumont de Courset reported in a letter to Thouin dated 25 July 1804 that he had successfully germinated 75 species, including members of the Proteaceae family. In return, he offered Thouin some of the plants he had successfully cultivated and passed on some of the seeds to the Italian botanist Domenico Nocca, professor at Pavia. These exchanges illustrate Thouin's central role in establishing and maintaining a European network for plant acclimatisation.

Thus, the botanical collections gathered along the coasts of mainland Australia and Van Diemen's Land, including eucalypts, acacias, melaleucas, banksias and hibiscuses, sustained a European network of horticultural experimentation and scientific exchange long after the return of the corvettes.

Thouin's contribution to this endeavour demonstrates the extent to which the scientific success of an expedition also depended on the work of scientists who had remained in France.
